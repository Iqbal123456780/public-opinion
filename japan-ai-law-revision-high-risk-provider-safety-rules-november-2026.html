Japan AI Law Revision Targets High-Risk Providers

Japan's working group has proposed stronger safety and reporting duties for selected high-risk AI service providers, but the draft is not yet law.

Published November 28, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Japan's Japan AI law revision debate advanced after a government working group published a draft outline on November 27. The proposal would give authorities stronger oversight tools for selected high-risk AI services, including safety duties, incident reporting and orders to correct serious problems. It is not yet a bill or an enacted law. The draft seeks to address risks from powerful systems while preserving space for research and commercial development. Its scope, thresholds and deadlines still need decisions.

What the Japan AI law revision would change

The Cabinet Office working group's draft proposes new rules for a limited group of AI service providers. Covered companies could have to assess serious risks, reduce them and report major incidents. Officials could request information, recommend corrective action and issue orders when the draft's conditions are met.

The paper is a draft summary, not final legislation. The Japan AI law revision therefore describes a policy direction under review, not a legal duty that companies must follow today. The working group has not published a final bill or compliance timetable.

Why Japan is revisiting its AI framework

Japan's AI Promotion Act was enacted in 2025. It sets broad principles, assigns responsibilities to government and businesses, and supports research and use of AI. The framework initially relied heavily on existing sector rules, guidance and voluntary cooperation.

The Cabinet Office says AI capabilities have advanced quickly. Its draft cites concerns about AI-assisted cyberattacks, dangerous misuse and systems behaving in unexpected ways. The Japan AI law revision aims to address risks that could affect public safety or essential services, not every error made by a consumer chatbot.

Which AI providers could be covered

The proposal does not seek to regulate every developer or company using an AI tool. It focuses on providers offering high-performance AI services that are widely used in Japan and could cause serious harm at scale. Computing power may help assess capability, but the draft sets no final numerical threshold. The Japan AI law revision would therefore focus on the services and their reach, not just a model's technical label.

Domestic use could determine reach

Usage inside Japan would also matter. A foreign provider with many Japanese users could potentially be covered if its service meets the final criteria. Companies offering services built on open-weight models may also fall within scope when their own services create comparable risks.

Safety duties would focus on serious risks

Covered providers could have to evaluate whether their services might cause major harm and take steps to prevent it. The draft gives examples such as technical safeguards against dangerous outputs, stronger internal controls and independent checks of safety systems.

Measures would depend on the service. A model used for medical support, software development or critical infrastructure may need different safeguards. The proposal leaves room for providers to choose suitable controls, while government guidance would set the expected standard.

Incident reporting could become mandatory

The current framework allows the government to investigate certain AI-related concerns, but the working group says cooperation can be voluntary. The draft proposes requiring covered providers to report when a service causes, or presents a concrete risk of causing, serious harm.

That could give authorities earlier notice of incidents involving cyber systems, critical services or public safety. The proposal does not yet set reporting deadlines or a complete list of reportable events. Those details must be defined before any duty can be enforced.

Government could request data and order fixes

The draft would let authorities request information about a provider's safety measures and operations when needed to assess compliance. It also discusses on-site inspections where ordinary reporting is insufficient, while stressing the need to protect trade secrets.

If a covered provider failed to meet its duties, officials could recommend corrective action and, in some cases, issue an order. The draft also considers emergency orders where serious harm appears imminent. These would be proposed future powers, not existing authorities.

Penalties would target failure to obey orders

The penalty proposal is narrower than a blanket punishment for unsafe AI output. It suggests sanctions when a covered provider fails to report a serious incident or disobeys a government order. The working group says penalties should be proportionate to other digital-sector laws.

The draft does not say every model error or inaccurate answer would automatically trigger a fine. The focus is on a defined group of providers and their response to serious risks and lawful instructions. A final bill would need to specify offences, defences and appeal rights.

Japan's AI Safety Institute may gain a bigger role

The working group proposes a stronger technical role for Japan's AI Safety Institute, or AISI. It could help evaluate model capabilities, assess risk controls and support officials reviewing reports or considering enforcement. The draft says the institute may need more staff and technical capacity.

Its governance would also matter. AISI works with industry and researchers, so any new enforcement role would need safeguards for independence and neutrality. Its expertise could help regulators avoid vague rules, but its future responsibilities remain under discussion.

How Japan's approach compares with other rules

The European Union's AI Act uses a broader risk-based structure, covering prohibited practices, general-purpose AI models, transparency and high-risk applications. The European Commission says several rules are already being enforced, while important high-risk requirements are scheduled for later phases in 2027 and 2028.

The United States has a more fragmented approach, with federal agencies using existing powers and states adopting their own rules. Japan's proposal would target a narrower group of providers. The Cabinet Office says international alignment should help prevent conflicting requirements for global companies.

What happens next for the proposal

The November 27 paper identifies policy choices but gives no final bill, compliance date or complete list of covered providers. The Cabinet Office and wider AI policy process must decide how to define high performance, domestic reach, reportable incidents and regulator powers.

Businesses should track the next official draft and review safety, incident-response and documentation systems. The Japan AI law revision could shape how high-impact services are governed, but its final effect will depend on the text lawmakers eventually consider. Clear thresholds and transparent enforcement will be essential to protect users without blocking useful research.

Five questions readers are asking

Has Japan passed the proposed new AI rules?

No. The November 27 document is a working-group draft summary, not an enacted law or final bill.

Which companies could be covered?

Providers of high-performance AI services widely used in Japan and capable of causing serious harm at scale.

Would every AI chatbot have to report incidents?

No. The draft points to a limited group of covered providers. Final thresholds and reporting triggers have not been set.

Could companies face penalties?

The draft proposes sanctions for failures to report or obey government orders, not automatic fines for every AI error.

Could foreign AI companies be affected?

Potentially, if their services are widely used in Japan and meet the final risk and capability criteria.
