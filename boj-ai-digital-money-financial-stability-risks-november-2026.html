BOJ Warns of AI Financial Stability Risks

A Bank of Japan speech warns that AI, stablecoins and tokenised deposits could create new cybersecurity and financial stability challenges.

Published November 27, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Digital finance is moving beyond payments into deposits, securities and cross-border settlement. Faster systems can also create new points of failure. In a November 27 speech in Tokyo, Bank of Japan Executive Director Kazushige Kamiyama urged policymakers to pair digital-money innovation with stronger safeguards. He discussed stablecoins, tokenised deposits, AI and the infrastructure connecting financial institutions. The speech announced no new rule or ban. It outlined challenges banks, technology providers and supervisors must manage as digital finance expands. These AI financial stability risks concern the systems around the technology, not just an AI tool's performance.

What did the Bank of Japan warn about?

In his November 27 keynote at an IMF seminar, Kamiyama examined how digital money is changing settlement. He said stablecoins and tokenised deposits are likely to coexist. Policymakers must make them work together without creating fragmentation or weakening financial stability.

AI can also make software vulnerabilities easier to discover and exploit. When payment platforms and providers are closely connected, an incident can spread quickly. Reuters described the speech as a call for stronger safeguards, not an immediate regulatory crackdown.

Why digital money is moving beyond experiments

Tokenisation represents money or assets as digital tokens on programmable ledgers. It can link a security transfer to payment, automate checks and support settlement beyond traditional banking hours. Kamiyama said financial institutions and public authorities are moving from experiments toward practical use.

Payments still depend on legal claims, liquidity, identity checks and reliable institutions. Faster settlement helps only when participants know what they own, when payment is final and who is responsible if a service fails.

Stablecoins and tokenised deposits are different

Kamiyama distinguished two forms of digital money. Stablecoins are privately issued tokens designed to track a reference currency. Tokenised deposits are commercial-bank deposits represented on a digital ledger, so the claim remains against a bank.

These forms do not offer identical protections. Their interaction is the policy challenge. If users cannot convert money reliably at face value, confidence may weaken during stress. The BOJ says central-bank money remains an anchor for trust and final settlement.

How artificial intelligence changes the cybersecurity picture

AI can help firms detect suspicious activity, review code and respond to incidents. It can also help attackers find weaknesses and adapt malicious activity. Kamiyama said AI's growing ability to discover and exploit vulnerabilities raises the stakes for digital-money infrastructure.

Security must be built into the system

Security covers more than a customer app. It includes smart-contract code, private-key management, cloud providers, identity systems and links between platforms. AI financial stability risks become harder to contain when institutions rely on the same technology or service provider.

Kamiyama did not say AI has caused a systemic payment failure. He warned that the threat environment is changing and that security must keep pace.

Why interconnected platforms could spread market stress

Tokenised markets may support around-the-clock trading and settlement. Smart contracts can trigger actions automatically, including margin calls or collateral sales. Kamiyama warned that these tools could amplify market moves if many participants react at once during stress.

Speed can reduce delays but leave less time to intervene. If liquidity is split across incompatible platforms, a firm may have funds in one place but need them elsewhere. Interoperability and liquidity planning matter as much as transaction speed. These AI financial stability risks can compound when automated systems react together.

Legal certainty matters as much as code

Tokens can move in seconds, but the legal rights behind them still need to be clear. Kamiyama asked whether transferring a token also transfers enforceable rights in the underlying asset. That question matters for securities, deposits and claims held through intermediaries.

Technical settlement is not always legal finality. A ledger may record a transfer while ownership or obligations remain disputed. Clear rules help courts, supervisors and users respond when a platform fails or a provider becomes insolvent.

What banks and payment firms should do

The BOJ speech does not create new legal requirements. But it points to practical priorities: test smart contracts, protect signing keys, monitor third-party providers and maintain recovery plans. Firms should understand how their systems connect with other networks.

Institutions should test automated processes under stress, including simultaneous collateral calls, outages and sudden withdrawals. AI financial stability risks cannot be handled by cybersecurity teams alone; treasury, legal, compliance and operations need a shared view.

Why cross-border coordination matters

Digital assets and payment platforms can cross borders while firms remain supervised under different national rules. The Financial Stability Board said in October that global stablecoins can create cross-border vulnerabilities that no single jurisdiction can assess alone.

The IMF's 2026 work on tokenised finance also stresses legal certainty, trusted settlement assets and coordination. Kamiyama cited Project Agorá, where central banks and private institutions are exploring shared approaches to cross-border payments. Technology may be global, but accountability must remain clear.

What consumers and businesses should watch

Consumers should ask who holds the money, how it can be redeemed, what happens if an app or issuer fails and where complaints go. A digital token is not automatically equivalent to a bank deposit or government-backed cash; protections depend on its issuer and legal structure.

Businesses should review payment-provider terms, data and infrastructure locations, continuity plans and the ability to move funds between systems. They should also ask how an automated transaction can be stopped or challenged. The AI financial stability risks are manageable only when users understand those safeguards.

What happens next for digital finance?

The BOJ will continue its DLT Sandbox Project, including work on cross-border payments, interbank settlement and tokenised Japanese government bonds. It is also participating in Project Agorá. The bank said no decision has been made to issue a retail digital currency.

The next test is whether new services can operate safely beside existing money and payment systems. AI financial stability risks are not a reason to stop innovation. They are a reminder that trust, cybersecurity and clear legal rights must develop with it.

Does the speech create a new ban?

No. It outlines risks and policy priorities, not a new prohibition.

Are stablecoins and tokenised deposits identical?

No. One is privately issued; the other represents a bank deposit.

Why mention AI?

Because it can help identify and exploit software vulnerabilities.

Can tokenisation increase market stress?

Yes. Automated collateral actions could amplify moves in some conditions.

Has Japan approved a retail digital yen?

No. The BOJ says no issuance decision has been made.

Conclusion: Digital money and AI may improve payments and settlement, but they need secure infrastructure and clear legal rights. The BOJ's message is that innovation should move forward without leaving public trust behind.
