BOJ Warns of AI Financial Stability Risks

A Bank of Japan speech warns that AI, stablecoins and tokenised deposits could create new cybersecurity and financial stability challenges.

Published November 27, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Financial technology is moving deeper into payments, deposits, securities and cross-border transfers. Yet faster systems can also create new points of failure. In a November 27 speech at an International Monetary Fund seminar in Tokyo, Bank of Japan Executive Director Kazushige Kamiyama urged policymakers to pair digital-money innovation with stronger safeguards. He discussed stablecoins, tokenised deposits, artificial intelligence and the infrastructure connecting financial institutions. The speech did not announce a new rule or ban. Instead, it set out challenges that banks, technology providers and supervisors must manage as digital finance expands. These AI financial stability risks concern the systems around the technology, not simply whether an AI tool works.

What did the Bank of Japan warn about?

Kamiyama's keynote, titled Digital Money and Payments: Transitioning Toward a New Paradigm, examined how new forms of money and financial assets are changing settlement. He said stablecoins and tokenised deposits are likely to coexist with other forms of money. The challenge is making those systems work together without creating unnecessary fragmentation or weakening financial stability.

He also warned that artificial intelligence can make it easier to discover and exploit software vulnerabilities. When payment platforms, banks and service providers are closely connected, an incident in one part of the network may spread quickly. Reuters reported the speech as a call for stronger global safeguards, not as an announcement of an immediate regulatory crackdown.

Why digital money is moving beyond experiments

Tokenisation means representing money or other assets as digital tokens on a programmable ledger. The technology can link a payment to the transfer of a security, automate checks and support settlement outside traditional banking hours. Kamiyama said financial institutions and public authorities are moving from small experiments toward practical applications.

That shift matters because payments are not isolated software transactions. They rely on legal claims, liquidity, identity checks, reliable infrastructure and institutions that can resolve disputes. A faster transaction is useful only if participants know what they own, when payment is final and who is responsible when something fails.

Stablecoins and tokenised deposits are different

The speech distinguishes two forms of digital money that can look similar to users but rest on different legal and financial foundations. Stablecoins are usually privately issued tokens designed to track a reference currency. Tokenised deposits are commercial-bank deposits represented in token form, so the underlying claim remains against a bank.

These forms do not carry identical risks or protections. Their interaction is the bigger policy question. If users cannot convert different forms of money reliably at face value, confidence can weaken during stress. The Bank of Japan argues that central-bank money remains an important anchor for trust and final settlement.

How artificial intelligence changes the cybersecurity picture

AI can help financial firms detect suspicious activity, review code and respond to incidents. The same capabilities can also help attackers search for weaknesses, automate reconnaissance or adapt malicious activity. Kamiyama said the growing ability of AI to identify and exploit vulnerabilities raises the stakes for institutions that operate digital-money infrastructure.

Security must be built into the system

For banks, the issue is not limited to protecting a customer app. It includes the code behind smart contracts, the management of private keys, cloud providers, identity systems and the links between platforms. AI financial stability risks become harder to contain when several institutions depend on the same technology or service provider.

The speech does not claim that AI has already caused a systemic failure in digital payments. It warns that the threat environment is changing, and that security controls must keep pace with new tools and more automated transactions.

Why interconnected platforms could spread market stress

Tokenised markets may support trading and settlement around the clock. Smart contracts can also trigger actions automatically, including margin calls or the sale of collateral. Kamiyama cautioned that these mechanisms could amplify market movements during periods of stress if many participants respond at the same time.

Speed can reduce delays, but it can also leave less time for people to understand what is happening and intervene. If liquidity is split across platforms that cannot easily communicate, a firm may have funds in one place but need them somewhere else. Interoperability and liquidity planning therefore matter as much as transaction speed.

Legal certainty matters as much as code

A digital token may move between platforms in seconds, but the legal rights behind it still need to be clear. Kamiyama raised a basic question: does transferring a token also transfer enforceable rights in the asset it represents? That issue matters for securities, deposits and other claims that may be held by several parties.

Technical settlement is not always the same as legal finality. A system may record a transfer as complete while a dispute remains over ownership, authority or the obligations of an intermediary. Clear rules help courts, supervisors and users understand what happens if a platform fails, a transaction is reversed or a service provider becomes insolvent.

What banks and payment firms should do

The BOJ speech is not a checklist of new legal requirements. But its analysis points to practical priorities for institutions considering tokenised services. Firms need to test smart contracts, protect signing keys, monitor third-party providers, maintain recovery plans and understand how their systems connect with other networks.

They should also test how automated processes behave during extreme conditions. That includes simultaneous collateral calls, outages, delayed settlement and sudden withdrawals. AI financial stability risks cannot be managed by cybersecurity teams alone; treasury, legal, compliance, operations and senior management all need a shared view of the exposures.

Why cross-border coordination matters

Digital assets and payment platforms can operate across borders even when the firms involved are supervised under different national rules. The Financial Stability Board said in October that global stablecoin arrangements can create vulnerabilities across countries and sectors, making it difficult for one jurisdiction to assess the full risk on its own.

The International Monetary Fund's 2026 work on tokenised finance also highlights the need for legal certainty, reliable settlement assets and coordination. Kamiyama pointed to international projects, including Project Agorá, as examples of central banks and private institutions exploring shared approaches to cross-border payments. The technology may be global, but responsibility still needs to be clear.

What consumers and businesses should watch

For consumers, the immediate questions are familiar: who holds the money, how can it be redeemed, what happens if an app or issuer fails, and where can a complaint be taken? A digital token should not be treated as automatically equivalent to a bank deposit or government-backed cash. The protections depend on the issuer, the legal structure and the rules in the relevant country.

Businesses should examine the terms of payment providers, the location of data and infrastructure, service continuity and the ability to move funds between systems. They should also ask how an automated transaction can be stopped or challenged. Clear answers can matter more than a promise of faster settlement.

What happens next for digital finance?

The Bank of Japan said it will continue practical work through its DLT Sandbox Project, including use cases for cross-border payments, interbank settlement and tokenised Japanese government bonds. It is also participating in Project Agorá. The central bank said no decision has been made to issue a retail central-bank digital currency.

The next stage will be judged by whether new services can operate safely alongside existing money and payment systems. The AI financial stability risks identified in the speech are not a reason to stop innovation. They are a reminder that trust, legal clarity, cybersecurity and sound institutions must develop with it.

Does the BOJ warning mean a new AI or crypto ban?

No. The November 27 speech described risks and policy priorities. It did not announce a new ban on AI, stablecoins or tokenised deposits.

Are stablecoins the same as tokenised bank deposits?

No. Stablecoins are privately issued tokens designed to track a reference value. Tokenised deposits represent claims on commercial banks.

Why did the BOJ mention artificial intelligence?

Kamiyama said AI can make it easier to discover and exploit software vulnerabilities, increasing the need for stronger cybersecurity.

Could tokenisation make financial markets more volatile?

It could create new channels for stress, especially if automated collateral calls and connected platforms amplify rapid market moves.

Has Japan decided to issue a retail digital yen?

No. The BOJ said its retail CBDC pilot continues, but no decision has been made to issue one.

Conclusion: Digital money and AI may improve payments, settlement and access to financial services. But the systems must be secure, interoperable and supported by clear legal rights. The Bank of Japan's message is straightforward: innovation can move quickly, but public trust must not be left behind.
