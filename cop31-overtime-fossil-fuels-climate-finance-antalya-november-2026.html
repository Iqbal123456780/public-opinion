COP31 Talks Go Overtime as Fossil Fuel Dispute Blocks Deal

Negotiators in Antalya remain divided over fossil fuels, climate finance and electrification as the UN summit extends beyond its scheduled finish.

Published: November 21, 2026, 8:00 AM PKT · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The COP31 climate talks in Antalya have gone beyond their scheduled finish as governments struggle to agree on a final text. The main dispute is whether the deal should explicitly call for a transition away from fossil fuels. A revised draft released by co-host Australia removed the language, prompting criticism from climate-vulnerable countries and campaigners.

The negotiations also cover climate finance, adaptation, carbon capture and a proposed global electrification target. As of this briefing, no final agreement has been confirmed. The COP31 climate talks remain a test of whether nearly 200 countries can find consensus on a politically sensitive package.

Why the summit has run into overtime

The summit's scheduled conclusion passed while negotiators continued working through unresolved issues. Climate conferences routinely extend beyond their official deadline when countries cannot agree on the final cover decision. This year's talks have been especially difficult because governments are balancing energy security, economic pressures and the costs of climate adaptation.

The COP31 climate talks extension does not mean the conference has failed. It means the draft remains unsettled and delegations are still negotiating. The latest reporting from Dawn/AFP and Associated Press describes talks continuing after the scheduled finish.

The revised draft removes fossil-fuel language

The most visible change is the removal of references to coal, oil and gas from a revised draft. Earlier versions included calls for accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels. The omission is significant because countries had agreed at COP28 to transition away from fossil fuels, but they have not agreed on a timetable or binding pathway.

Supporters of stronger language say the final decision should preserve momentum and give governments a clear direction. Opponents of a phase-out text argue that national energy systems and development needs differ. The revised draft is a negotiating document, not the final agreement, and its wording could still change.

Countries demand a clearer transition pathway

A group of countries has called for language that explicitly supports a transition away from fossil fuels. Panama and several European and developing nations have pushed for stronger wording, while Panama's environment minister has warned that the country may object to a weak deal. The dispute highlights how consensus rules can give individual governments leverage.

Negotiators are trying to find wording that acknowledges the need to reduce emissions without prescribing the same timetable for every country. The final text may include a roadmap, a voluntary pledge or a broader transition phrase. As of this briefing, no compromise has been confirmed.

Electrification target offers a possible compromise

The draft promotes increasing the share of final energy demand met by electricity to 35% by 2035. Electrification can reduce direct fossil-fuel use in transport, heating and industry, but its climate benefit depends on how electricity is generated. A grid powered by coal or gas can limit the emissions savings.

Some governments want the target linked explicitly to renewable or clean energy. Others support electrification as a broader energy-security measure. Reuters has reported that the debate also touches growing demand from electric vehicles and AI data centres, illustrating how climate policy intersects with technology and industrial strategy.

Climate finance remains a major fault line

Developing countries say they need predictable finance to adapt to extreme weather, protect infrastructure and transition their economies. Negotiators have debated funding for adaptation and the wider climate-finance commitments made by wealthier nations. The latest drafts have left some countries dissatisfied with the scale and clarity of support.

The issue is not only how much money is promised, but how it is delivered. Governments want to know whether funds will be grants, loans or private investment, and whether they can reach communities facing urgent risks. Climate-vulnerable countries argue that vague declarations without resources cannot produce practical results.

The 1.5-degree goal is under pressure

The draft acknowledges concern that the Paris Agreement's 1.5-degree warming threshold may be exceeded. That warning reflects the gap between current emissions pathways and the reductions scientists say are needed to limit severe climate impacts. Acknowledging the risk does not replace measures that reduce emissions.

Countries disagree over how the summit should respond. Some want stronger mitigation language and faster fossil-fuel reductions, while others emphasize adaptation, energy access and national circumstances. The final text's credibility will depend on whether it connects scientific warnings with actions governments can implement.

Carbon capture and other contested technologies

Carbon capture has emerged as another disputed element. Some governments see it as a tool for reducing emissions in hard-to-abate sectors, while critics worry that it could be used to justify continued fossil-fuel production. The technology's cost, scale and commercial maturity remain important questions.

The debate illustrates why negotiators struggle to agree on a single pathway. A reference to carbon capture may help some countries accept a deal, but others want clearer priority for renewables, efficiency and direct emissions reductions. Any final language will need to balance those concerns without overstating what technology can deliver.

Energy security and climate diplomacy are colliding

The negotiations are taking place amid geopolitical tensions and concerns about energy prices. Governments are reluctant to accept commitments that they believe could threaten domestic supply or raise costs. That creates pressure to frame climate action as a way to strengthen energy security, not simply as an environmental obligation.

Electrification, renewables, grid upgrades and efficiency can reduce exposure to fuel-price shocks over time. However, the transition requires investment, planning and reliable infrastructure. The COP31 climate talks are therefore addressing economic and security questions as much as emissions targets.

Consensus rules shape the final agreement

UN climate decisions generally require consensus, giving governments an incentive to keep language acceptable to a wide range of countries. That can produce compromise texts that are less ambitious than individual delegations want. It can also prevent a deal from being adopted when disagreements remain unresolved.

The process is not a simple vote between a majority and a minority. Negotiators must work through language, bracketed paragraphs and competing proposals. The final document may include voluntary initiatives, reporting requirements or broad political signals, but its strength will depend on whether countries can agree to them.

What negotiators still need to resolve

The remaining issues in the COP31 climate talks include fossil-fuel language, climate finance, adaptation support, the electrification target and the role of carbon capture. Countries may seek a package deal in which concessions on one topic help unlock agreement on another. As talks continue, new drafts can alter the balance of the text.

A final agreement has not been confirmed at the time of this briefing. Readers should distinguish the latest draft from an adopted decision. The UNFCCC's official COP31 page provides the conference context and official updates.

Why the outcome matters beyond Antalya

A weak agreement could disappoint countries and communities already facing heat, floods, drought and rising seas. A stronger text could help governments coordinate investment and policy, although a diplomatic statement alone cannot cut emissions. The real test comes after the summit, when countries turn commitments into national plans and funding.

The COP31 climate talks will be judged by whether they produce clear, measurable steps on energy, finance and adaptation. A final deal may not resolve every dispute, but it can shape the next round of climate diplomacy. Until the adopted text is released, the outcome remains uncertain.
