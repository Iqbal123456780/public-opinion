Oxfam: Gaza Contamination Risks Cover 99% of Territory

A new Oxfam and CEOBS analysis warns of widespread pollution indicators. The 99% figure is a spatial risk finding, not a soil test from every location.

Published November 24, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

A new analysis by Oxfam and the Conflict and Environment Observatory warns that potential contamination indicators cover more than 99 percent of Gaza's territory. Released on November 23, the report identifies possible risks from explosive residues, heavy metals, asbestos, sewage and other pollutants. Its findings raise urgent questions about drinking water, food production, debris removal and public health as reconstruction planning continues. The headline figure needs careful explanation: it comes from spatial analysis using satellite imagery, remote sensing, scientific research and open-source data. It does not mean that laboratory teams tested every parcel of land. The Gaza contamination risks are widespread, but their exact severity in each location still requires safe access and detailed field assessment.

A New Report Puts Environmental Damage at the Center of Recovery

Oxfam and the Conflict and Environment Observatory released the report on November 23. It examines how military activity, damaged infrastructure, fires, rubble and disrupted environmental systems may have released hazardous substances into air, soil and water. The authors argue that contamination must be addressed alongside emergency relief and reconstruction.

The report adds an environmental dimension to a crisis often discussed through displacement, casualties and destroyed buildings. Pollution can persist after fighting stops and complicate rebuilding if workers disturb hazardous materials. The authors say Gaza contamination risks must shape recovery planning.

What the 99 Percent Figure Actually Means

The report's spatial analysis identified at least one potential contamination indicator across more than 99 percent of Gaza's territory. That is a finding about the geographical spread of potential risks. It does not mean that 99 percent of the land has been laboratory-tested or that every location has the same level of pollution.

A map can identify where contamination may be likely based on damage and known incidents. It cannot replace soil, water and air sampling. Field assessment is needed to establish the type, concentration and exposure risk in specific neighbourhoods.

How Researchers Built the Assessment

The analysis combines satellite imagery, remote sensing, scientific literature and open-source conflict data. The report says incident datasets from groups including ACLED, Airwars and GeoConfirmed were geolocated and assessed alongside geospatial information. Researchers used these sources to identify military activity that could release pollutants into the environment.

Remote sensing helps when teams cannot safely reach affected areas. It can show damaged buildings, fires, disturbed land and wastewater outfalls. But some contaminants are invisible from space, and imagery cannot determine chemical concentrations. The report is a risk picture, not laboratory testing.

The Contaminants Include Metals, Explosive Residues and Asbestos

The report identifies several possible contaminant groups. They include heavy metals, explosive residues and perchlorates, asbestos fibres, crystalline silica, persistent organic pollutants and biological pathogens. Sources can include destroyed buildings, industrial and energy facilities, weapons, damaged solar panels, waste sites and broken sanitation systems.

These materials behave differently. Some move in dust, others seep into soil or groundwater, and some remain in debris until disturbed. Risk depends on the substance, concentration, location and exposure route. Cleanup therefore needs more than one waste-removal method.

Damaged Water and Sanitation Systems Create Exposure Paths

Water and sanitation are central to the environmental assessment. The report documents wastewater outfalls and the long-running discharge of untreated sewage into coastal waters. Satellite observations identified an outfall near the Deir al-Balah displacement camp that remained visibly active for at least 16 consecutive months, from April 2024 to August 2025.

Sewage can carry pathogens into surface water, groundwater and coastal areas. These are among the Gaza contamination risks researchers want to measure. When treatment plants and pipes fail, families have fewer safe options for drinking, washing and irrigation. The report links these pathways to disease and food security. Sampling is needed to measure local conditions.

Rubble Creates Dust, Chemical and Unexploded-Ordnance Hazards

The report cites a United Nations Environment Programme estimate of 61 million tonnes of debris in Gaza. Destroyed buildings can contain asbestos, concrete dust, metals, plastics and other materials. The rubble may also conceal unexploded ordnance, which can prevent safe access for residents, engineers and environmental teams.

Debris removal is not simply construction. Workers must identify hazardous materials, separate waste streams and manage unexploded ordnance before recycling or disposal. Poorly planned clearance can spread dust or move pollutants. Safe sorting is a major part of recovery.

Children and Pregnant Women May Face Particular Health Risks

People may encounter pollutants through the air they breathe, water and food they consume, or direct contact with soil and debris. Dust can rise when damaged buildings are cleared. Contaminated water may be used for washing or irrigation when safer supplies are limited. The report warns that children and pregnant women can face higher risks.

The assessment does not measure illness caused by each contaminant. Health outcomes depend on dose, duration and individual vulnerability. Agencies will need sampling, disease surveillance and accessible medical care. The report describes exposure pathways, not a diagnosis of every resident.

Israel's Embassy Disputes the Report's Methodology

The Israeli embassy in the United Kingdom questioned the report's reliance on satellite imagery and what it described as unverified assumptions. It also argued that the analysis did not adequately examine the impact of underground tunnels in Gaza. The embassy said that omission undermined the report's credibility, according to The Guardian.

Oxfam and CEOBS identify potential contamination and call for access to conduct detailed assessment. Independent fieldwork is therefore important. The findings are not a final legal ruling or a substitute for laboratory evidence. Methods and assumptions should remain open to scrutiny.

Cleanup Requires Access, Testing and Careful Reconstruction

The report recommends reducing exposure while assessment proceeds. Priorities include safe access for specialists, laboratory testing, ordnance removal, water repairs and careful debris sorting. Mapping Gaza contamination risks must guide where teams work first. Urgent recovery need not wait for every scientific question, but permanent rebuilding needs stronger evidence and safeguards.

UNEP's earlier assessments also warned that debris, damaged wastewater systems and contaminated land could affect health and food production for years. Recovery will require coordination between local authorities, humanitarian agencies, engineers and environmental scientists. The cost and timetable will depend on access, security, equipment and the ability to test samples safely.

Conclusion: Environmental Assessment Must Be Part of Gaza's Recovery

The Oxfam and CEOBS report warns of widespread potential pollutants. Its 99 percent figure is a spatial indicator, not a laboratory test of every site. Debris, damaged sanitation and possible chemical contamination still point to serious environmental challenges.

Gaza contamination risks require independent field assessment and practical exposure reduction. Rebuilding without addressing water quality, hazardous rubble and soil contamination could create new health problems. Gaza contamination risks must be part of recovery from the start.
