New Provinces in Pakistan: Why the Sindh Debate Is Dividing Political Leaders

A proposed referendum and calls for smaller administrative units have reopened questions about Sindh, Karachi, constitutional authority and the cost of restructuring Pakistan.

Published: November 19, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team · Updated: November 19, 2026

Pakistan’s debate over new provinces in Pakistan has moved from political speeches into a national argument about power, identity and public services. Recent remarks by senior leaders have brought Sindh and Karachi into sharp focus, while proposals for smaller administrative units have raised questions across the country. For readers in the United States, the issue may seem like an internal boundary dispute. It reaches much further. The debate touches Pakistan’s federal structure, constitutional safeguards, resource sharing and the balance between local control and national unity. It also highlights disputes over how citizens receive effective public services. Here is what happened, what the law says and why the discussion matters now.

What is being proposed and why now?

Pakistan has four provinces: Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan. Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi has advocated smaller administrative units. Defence Minister Khawaja Asif has publicly discussed a possible expansion, while Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal has mentioned 12 to 15 provinces through consensus.

These remarks have not produced a formal bill, an agreed number of provinces or an official timetable. The current debate over new provinces in Pakistan remains at the discussion stage. Supporters argue that smaller units could bring public services closer to residents. Critics ask whether redrawing boundaries would fix weak administration without deeper institutional reform. Al Jazeera’s explainer reports that no governing-party agreement has been announced.

How can Pakistan create new provinces?

The Constitution does not provide a shortcut for creating provinces. Article 239 governs constitutional amendments. A proposed amendment needs support from at least two-thirds of the total membership of both houses of Parliament before it can proceed to the president.

If the change alters a province’s boundaries, Article 239(4) also requires approval by at least two-thirds of that province’s assembly. Any plan for new provinces in Pakistan must meet this provincial threshold. For Sindh, that means the provincial assembly must approve the boundary change. Could a referendum decide the issue?

Mohsin Naqvi has proposed asking the public whether Pakistan should create additional provinces. MQM-P leader Farooq Sattar has also called for a nationwide referendum before 2027. Supporters say a public vote could help political parties understand what citizens want.

However, a referendum cannot simply replace the constitutional amendment process. The referendum debate over new provinces in Pakistan must therefore be understood alongside Parliament’s constitutional role. Al Jazeera reports that Article 48(6) provides a route for referring a national question to voters after the required parliamentary step. Legal experts cited in that report say a referendum would not itself redraw provincial boundaries or replace Article 239.

How much would new provinces cost, and what about money and water?

New provinces would need assemblies, secretariats, courts, police services and senior administrative offices. Those institutions require both start-up funding and annual budgets. A Policy Research Institute of Market Economy estimate cited by Al Jazeera put the annual cost of dividing Punjab into three provinces at Rs46.9 billion to Rs56.2 billion.

Money and water raise further questions. The financial plan for new provinces in Pakistan would need to explain how existing revenue and water agreements would work after any boundary change. The National Finance Commission distributes federal tax resources among provinces, while the 1991 Water Apportionment Accord governs sharing from the Indus system. Existing rules do not fully explain how these arrangements would work after a province splits. Any plan needs clear answers before boundaries change.

Where do the PML-N and PPP stand?

The PPP governs Sindh and has rejected dividing the province. In September 2026, the Sindh Assembly adopted a resolution against the province’s division. President Asif Ali Zardari has also reiterated the party’s opposition, according to reporting on his meeting with Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif.

The PML-N has not announced a settled formal position on the proposed map. MQM-P leaders, by contrast, have pressed for new administrative units and stronger local authority, including a separate administrative status for Karachi. The coalition therefore contains different views, not a single agreed plan.

Opposition to referendum

Opposition to a referendum comes from concerns about constitutional procedure and political purpose. The PPP argues that the Constitution already sets out how boundaries can change. It also says the people of Sindh and their elected representatives have already expressed opposition to dividing the province.

MQM-P takes a different position. Farooq Sattar says citizens should have a direct say in the country’s administrative structure. The disagreement is not simply about whether people should be heard. It is about how public opinion should fit alongside parliamentary and provincial powers.

Acknowledging mistakes

Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari has called for a broader political reset, arguing that political leaders should address governance failures rather than turn every national problem into a boundary dispute. His comments link the provinces debate with inflation, poverty, unemployment and public confidence.

That argument deserves scrutiny from every party. New boundaries cannot automatically improve schools, hospitals or local services. Better results also depend on capable institutions, transparent budgets, accountable officials and elected local governments with real authority.

Soft corner for opposition

Bilawal has also spoken about restoring political dialogue with opposition parties. This matters because constitutional amendments require broad parliamentary support. A process that excludes major political groups could struggle to gain the votes needed for lasting reform.

Political dialogue does not require parties to abandon their positions. It requires them to explain their proposals, answer criticism and work through lawful institutions. The public can then compare the arguments rather than rely only on slogans or speculation.

Tarar’s response

Federal Information Minister Attaullah Tarar urged Bilawal to raise disagreements within the governing coalition. His response reflects the pressure on coalition partners to manage public differences while maintaining cooperation on government business.

That tension is important for the provinces debate. Any constitutional proposal would need political negotiation across the federal government and the affected provincial assembly. Public statements alone cannot deliver the required votes or settle questions about funding and administration.

‘27th Amendment passed with brute majority’

Bilawal’s criticism of the 27th Amendment forms part of his wider argument about constitutional consensus. He has questioned whether major institutional changes should move forward without wider political consultation. Those comments should be read as his political position, not as a neutral description of every party’s motives.

The amendment debate is related to, but separate from, creating new provinces. The legal test for changing provincial boundaries remains Article 239. Readers should distinguish the text of enacted legislation from statements made by politicians about how it was passed.

‘JUI-F chief has right to criticise govt’

Bilawal has defended the right of opposition figures, including JUI-F chief Maulana Fazlur Rehman, to criticise government decisions. That position reflects a basic feature of parliamentary politics: opposition parties can challenge policy and demand explanations.

Criticism should remain peaceful and evidence-based. At the same time, governments should answer serious questions with facts, records and clear policy reasoning. Healthy debate is especially important when proposals could alter provincial identity and the distribution of public resources.

‘Federal govt must decide on reset’

The federal government now faces a choice about whether to turn public discussion into a formal policy process. That would require a written proposal, consultations with coalition partners, provincial governments and opposition parties, plus a clear constitutional route.

For now, no final map or approved timetable has been published in the reporting reviewed for this article. The phrase new provinces in Pakistan describes an active political debate, not a completed change. Watch for draft legislation, assembly resolutions and official announcements before treating any proposal as settled.

What could smaller provinces mean for ordinary people?

Supporters say smaller provinces could make officials more accessible and improve decisions about schools, hospitals, roads and water. Residents in distant districts might find it easier to reach provincial institutions. Those benefits, however, depend on genuine authority and adequate funding.

There are risks too. New capitals and departments could increase costs, while disputes over language, identity, jobs and revenue could deepen political tensions. The best test is practical: would a proposed structure deliver better services, fairer representation and transparent spending?

Frequently Asked Questions

How many provinces does Pakistan have now?

Pakistan has four provinces: Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan. Islamabad is a federal capital territory. Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir have separate constitutional and administrative arrangements.

Is Pakistan officially creating new provinces in 2026?

Political leaders have discussed additional provinces and administrative units, but the reports reviewed for this article do not show an approved constitutional amendment, agreed map or final timetable.

Can Karachi become a separate province?

Karachi has featured in proposals for a new administrative unit. However, changing Sindh’s boundaries would require the constitutional approvals set out in Article 239, including a two-thirds vote in the Sindh Assembly.

What does Article 239 say about creating new provinces?

Article 239 sets out the process for constitutional amendments. A boundary change also requires approval by at least two-thirds of the total membership of the affected provincial assembly.

Can a referendum create a new province by itself?

No. A referendum may be used to seek public opinion through the constitutional route, but it does not replace the amendment and provincial approval requirements for changing boundaries.

Would smaller provinces improve Pakistan’s economy?

They could bring some decisions closer to local communities, but they would also create administrative costs and complex questions about revenue, water and representation. The outcome would depend on the design and quality of governance.
