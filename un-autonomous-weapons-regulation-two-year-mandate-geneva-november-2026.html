UN Extends Autonomous Weapons Talks to 2028

UN states agreed to develop an instrument on lethal autonomous weapons by 2028, but the Geneva conference did not launch immediate binding treaty negotiations.

Published November 22, 2026, 8:00 AM Pakistan time · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Countries at a United Nations conference in Geneva agreed to continue work on rules for lethal autonomous weapons systems over the next two years. The decision creates a process to develop an instrument by 2028, but it did not secure an immediate mandate to negotiate a legally binding treaty.

The autonomous weapons regulation debate has gained urgency as militaries develop systems that can identify, select or engage targets with reduced human involvement. The outcome was welcomed as progress by some governments, while the International Committee of the Red Cross said the conference fell short of the binding rules it had urged.

Geneva conference ends with a two-year mandate

The Seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons concluded after five days of discussions on lethal autonomous weapon systems. Switzerland's foreign ministry said states agreed to develop an instrument aimed at regulating their use over the 2027–2028 period.

The agreement provides a new work programme after more than a decade of discussions. It does not settle the content or legal status of the future instrument. The autonomous weapons regulation process will now move into drafting and further negotiations among participating states.

What autonomous weapons systems can do

Lethal autonomous weapons are systems that can select and engage targets without a human making each individual decision. They may combine sensors, software, communications and weapons platforms. The level of autonomy varies, and not every AI-enabled military system is an autonomous weapon.

The central policy question is how much human judgment and control should remain over the use of force. Governments and experts debate whether a human must approve every strike, supervise a system or be able to intervene before an engagement. Definitions matter because they determine which systems future rules cover.

The new process aims to draft an instrument by 2028

The Swiss government said the agreed process would elaborate an instrument regulating lethal autonomous weapons over the next two years. Reuters reported that states will continue discussions with a view to developing rules by 2028. The agreement is a step beyond an open-ended exchange of views.

However, the mandate leaves the legal form of the instrument unresolved. A future document could include prohibitions, restrictions, principles or other measures. The autonomous weapons regulation process will need to address both technical definitions and obligations that governments can implement.

A binding treaty was not launched immediately

The International Committee of the Red Cross said the review conference failed to agree on a clear and immediate negotiation mandate for a legally binding instrument. The organisation had urged states to establish prohibitions and restrictions that preserve meaningful human control over force.

That distinction is important. A two-year process to develop an instrument does not automatically guarantee a treaty with enforceable obligations. Governments will still need to agree on the document's scope, legal status, timelines and how compliance could be assessed.

Countries remain divided over the legal approach

Some states and humanitarian organisations have argued for binding international rules, including prohibitions on weapons that cannot be used consistently with international humanitarian law. Others have favoured non-binding guidelines or argued that existing laws of armed conflict can address many concerns.

The review conference works under a consensus-based framework, which makes compromise difficult. The Reuters report said major military powers opposed an immediate legally binding pact, while a group of states supported stronger rules. The autonomous weapons regulation issue therefore remains tied to national security and diplomatic priorities.

Human control is central to humanitarian law

The ICRC says weapons must be used in ways that comply with international humanitarian law, including distinction, proportionality and precautions in attack. Autonomous systems can raise concerns if their behaviour is unpredictable or if operators cannot understand why a target was selected.

Human control is not simply a button pressed before launch. Experts debate what level of oversight is meaningful, how operators can monitor a system and whether they have enough time and information to intervene. The future instrument may need to define these requirements in practical terms.

Accountability cannot disappear into software

When a weapon causes unlawful harm, responsibility must be assessed through the people and institutions that developed, deployed, operated and commanded it. AI systems do not replace the legal duties of states or individuals. Clear records, testing and operational limits can help investigators determine what happened.

The autonomous weapons regulation discussions will need to consider how responsibility is assigned when a system behaves unexpectedly. That includes the design process, training data, testing conditions, command decisions and the circumstances in which the weapon was used.

Military AI development is moving faster than diplomacy

Autonomous systems are being developed alongside drones, sensor networks, software-assisted targeting and other military technologies. The combination can shorten the time between detecting a potential target and making an engagement decision. It can also increase the volume of information that operators must process.

The pace of development makes international rules difficult to write. Technologies change, and countries may not agree on which capabilities should be covered. A durable framework would need definitions that remain useful as systems evolve, while still setting clear limits on unacceptable uses.

Consensus rules make negotiations difficult

The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons requires consensus for many decisions. That structure allows states to participate in a common process, but it can give any delegation the ability to block stronger language. The conference has faced this obstacle during previous discussions on autonomous weapons.

Some countries may therefore consider pursuing rules through another diplomatic forum if progress stalls. A separate process could move faster, but it may also have fewer participating states. The challenge is to build rules that are widely accepted and meaningful in practice.

The next two years will determine the instrument's scope

The agreed process is expected to continue through 2027 and 2028. States will need to define the systems covered, decide whether the instrument is legally binding, identify prohibited or restricted uses and establish safeguards for human judgment and accountability.

The outcome will depend on national positions, technical discussions and humanitarian concerns. The autonomous weapons regulation debate has moved into a new phase, but the Geneva agreement is only a starting point. Publicly available drafts and meeting reports will show whether the process produces enforceable protections or a more limited set of principles.
