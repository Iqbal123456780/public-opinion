Taiwan Local Elections Point to DPP Gains

Early counts point to gains for Taiwan's ruling Democratic Progressive Party, but the opposition Kuomintang appears positioned to retain Taipei as local results continue to come in.

Published November 28, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The Taiwan local elections are drawing attention because the latest developments could affect politics, markets, security or technology well beyond the immediate location. Early counts point to gains for Taiwan's ruling Democratic Progressive Party, but the opposition Kuomintang appears positioned to retain Taipei as local results continue to come in. This report separates confirmed facts from official claims and forward-looking assessments. It uses current reporting and primary sources available when the article was prepared, and identifies where information remains incomplete.

What the early vote count shows

Initial counts indicated that the ruling Democratic Progressive Party could improve its position across several local contests, Reuters reported. In the six largest municipalities, the DPP appeared likely to win three, the same number as the opposition Kuomintang. The most notable possible gain was Taichung, Taiwan's second most populous city. The DPP also looked set to regain Yilan, Changhua and Hsinchu counties.

The picture was mixed rather than a nationwide sweep. The KMT remained competitive in several cities and appeared likely to retain Taipei. The Taiwan local elections therefore offered no simple verdict on the island's political direction. Vote totals were still being processed, and candidates had not declared victory or conceded in the six largest city races when Reuters filed its report.

Taipei remains the contest to watch

Taipei's mayoral race was one of the most closely followed contests. Incumbent KMT mayor Chiang Wan-an led DPP challenger Puma Shen in the early count, Reuters reported. Shen is an academic-turned-lawmaker whom Beijing has repeatedly denounced as a separatist. That criticism gave the contest a cross-strait dimension, although campaign debates in the capital also centred on transport, housing, public services and the environment.

A large local vote with national reach

Taiwan's Central Election Commission scheduled elections across all 22 cities and counties. The contests cover mayors, county magistrates, local councillors, township leaders and village chiefs. The commission said 11,051 local officials were to be elected, with about 19.65 million eligible voters. It is a vast exercise in local democracy, involving thousands of polling places and many separate races.

These elections matter because local governments control services people encounter every day. They oversee transport planning, waste management, parks, local roads, public health and parts of education policy. The result can reshape local budgets and political networks. It also shows which parties can recruit candidates, organise volunteers and persuade voters outside national campaigns. That is why the Taiwan local elections attract attention well beyond the mayoral contests.

Domestic issues dominated the campaign

Reuters reported that candidates in the island's 22 cities and counties focused mainly on local issues rather than the disputed relationship with Beijing. Transport congestion, environmental protection, housing and the quality of municipal services shaped many debates. Those issues often decide Taiwan local elections because voters can directly compare promises with their daily experience.

What the results mean for President Lai

President Lai Ching-te leads the DPP government, so gains by the ruling party would provide a political lift. Local elections are often treated as a midterm test of public confidence, even though they do not change the national presidency or legislature. A stronger DPP showing could help the party argue that voters still support its leadership and policy direction.

A weaker result would give the KMT and other opposition groups fresh material for criticism. Yet local contests are not a direct poll on every national policy. Candidate quality, local alliances and municipal performance can outweigh national approval ratings. The final pattern across cities and counties will matter more than a single headline result.

China watched, but the ballot was local

Beijing follows Taiwan's elections because the island's political leadership affects cross-strait relations. China claims Taiwan as part of its territory, while Taiwan's government rejects Beijing's sovereignty claim and says the island's future must be decided by its people. The political debate is sensitive, but Reuters reported that local issues dominated this campaign.

Security concerns remain in the background

Taiwan continues to face pressure from China's military activity around the island. The government has expanded defence spending and argued that deterrence is necessary to preserve peace. Beijing says its actions respond to what it calls separatist activity and foreign interference. Those positions remain sharply opposed, and local candidates have limited control over the national security debate.

For voters, security is one concern among many. A local administration must still manage schools, roads, emergency services and budgets regardless of tensions across the Taiwan Strait. That is why national security did not dominate every campaign meeting. The Taiwan local elections may influence the political climate in Taipei, but they do not replace the national institutions responsible for defence and diplomacy.

The road to 2028 starts in local government

The next presidential and legislative elections are due in 2028. Local victories can help parties build a bench of experienced politicians, strengthen campaign networks and test messages before that national contest. Mayors and county leaders can become influential voices inside their parties, while successful councillors often move into higher office.

What preliminary results can and cannot tell us

Early vote counts are useful because they show the direction of a race, but they are not the same as certified results. Counts may change as more polling stations report, and close contests can remain uncertain until the final ballots are included. Reuters said none of the candidates in the six largest cities had claimed victory or conceded when its report was published.

Readers should check the Central Election Commission's official results portal for updated totals and final declarations. Media projections can help explain the emerging picture, but the commission is the authority for official results. The Taiwan local contests deserve careful reporting that separates early leads, media projections and certified outcomes.

What happens after the count

Once the Central Election Commission confirms the results, winning candidates will turn from campaigning to transition planning. New mayors and county magistrates will prepare budgets, review existing projects and appoint local teams. Councils will also begin work on the next round of local spending and oversight. Those decisions will determine whether campaign promises become funded programmes.
