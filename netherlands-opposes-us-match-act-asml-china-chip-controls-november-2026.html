Dutch Push Back Against US MATCH Act Targeting ASML

By Public Opinion News Editorial Team · Published November 25, 2026

The Netherlands is working to stop a U.S. bill that could tighten restrictions on Dutch chip-equipment maker ASML's business in China, Foreign Minister Tom Berendsen said during a visit to Beijing. The proposed Multilateral Alignment of Technology Controls on Hardware Act, known as the US MATCH Act, would pressure allies such as the Netherlands and Japan to align export controls or face unilateral U.S. restrictions. The dispute is about more than one company's sales. It concerns who sets export rules, how allies coordinate technology security and whether restrictions could accelerate China's efforts to build its own chipmaking equipment.

What the Netherlands said in Beijing

Berendsen told reporters that his government's focus was to prevent the legislation from passing. He said the Netherlands did not want unilateral decisions with extraterritorial effects and would make its own independent choices on export controls. The remarks were reported by Reuters on Wednesday. They mark a clear public objection from a close U.S. security partner at a time when Washington is seeking tighter coordination with allies over advanced semiconductor equipment. The Dutch position does not mean the government opposes every export restriction. The disagreement concerns the reach and enforcement of this proposal.

What the bill would change

The Multilateral Alignment of Technology Controls on Hardware Act was introduced in the U.S. House as H.R. 8170 and in the Senate as S. 4281. The official bill records describe the measure as a proposal to restrict exports of certain semiconductor manufacturing equipment and related components. Reuters reported that Congress is debating whether to attach it to the annual National Defense Authorization Act. That process is not the same as enactment. The US MATCH Act has not become law, and its final text or legislative path could still change.

Why ASML sits at the center of the dispute

ASML is the world's leading supplier of lithography systems used to print patterns on silicon wafers. Its machines are essential to modern chip production. The proposed restrictions could affect deep ultraviolet immersion systems, known as DUV, and the servicing of equipment already installed at Chinese facilities. Reuters reported that ASML expects China to account for about 20% of its sales this year. That estimate makes the market commercially important, even as the company operates under existing export-control rules.

DUV and EUV tools serve different roles

Lithography systems use light to transfer extremely small patterns onto silicon wafers. ASML's extreme ultraviolet systems use light with a wavelength of 13.5 nanometres and are used for the most advanced manufacturing steps. Immersion DUV tools use a different light source and remain important for a wide range of chips. The distinction matters because existing restrictions already limit China's access to the most advanced equipment. The US MATCH Act would seek to extend controls to additional DUV tools and related services. It is not accurate to say every ASML product is currently banned from China.

Why Washington wants allied alignment

U.S. lawmakers argue that export controls work less effectively when companies in allied countries can supply equipment that American firms are barred from selling. The US MATCH Act is designed to reduce those gaps by encouraging partner governments to adopt similar restrictions. Supporters frame the approach as a national-security measure aimed at slowing China's ability to manufacture advanced chips for military and artificial-intelligence uses. They also argue that coordinated rules can prevent companies from shifting purchases to suppliers in countries with looser controls. Those arguments are policy claims, not proof that every restricted tool would have a military use.

The Dutch sovereignty argument

The Netherlands says export controls should be decided through its own national process and in coordination with partners, rather than imposed through a U.S. law with effects on Dutch companies. Dutch lawmakers have warned that the proposal could harm ASML and the country's wider technology economy. The debate reflects a difficult balance: the Netherlands relies on the United States for security cooperation, but it also hosts a company that occupies a critical position in global semiconductor supply chains. A public disagreement does not mean the two governments have ended their broader partnership.

China's market matters to the semiconductor business

ASML's China exposure is significant, but it is not the only driver of the company's outlook. In its third-quarter 2026 results, ASML reported total net sales of €11.7 billion and said it expected full-year sales of €44 billion to €45 billion. The company also described strong demand linked to AI-related investment. A tighter export regime could affect sales, servicing revenue and customer relationships, while restrictions could also change where chipmakers build capacity. The 20% China-sales estimate cited by Reuters is a forecast, not a guarantee of future revenue.

The servicing question could be as important as sales

A machine installed in a factory needs maintenance, spare parts, software updates and technical support. Restrictions on servicing can reduce a supplier's visibility into how equipment is used, but they can also leave customers searching for alternative repair options. That could affect safety, reliability and the pace of production. ASML has argued in earlier public comments that cutting off service could encourage customers to seek other providers or develop competing capabilities. Those are the company's arguments; the long-term effect of a new rule remains uncertain.

What the bill means for AI and supply chains

Advanced chips support data centres, cloud services, scientific computing and many AI systems. Export rules can therefore affect more than semiconductor manufacturers. They can influence the cost and location of computing capacity, the availability of components and the ability of companies to maintain equipment. A fragmented regulatory system may force manufacturers to design different supply chains for different markets. The US MATCH Act is one attempt to align restrictions, but the dispute shows that allies may disagree about how to balance security, trade and technological competition.

What happens next: Congress, ASML and FAQs

The next test is whether lawmakers attach the proposal to the defense bill or pursue it separately. The Dutch government will continue its diplomatic effort, while ASML and other suppliers will monitor the scope of any final legislation. The House and Senate bill records provide the formal text, but only an enacted law and subsequent regulations would establish binding obligations. Has the US MATCH Act become law?

No. It remains proposed legislation, and Congress is considering how to move it forward.

Which company is most directly affected?

ASML is central because it supplies lithography equipment, including immersion DUV systems.

What is DUV lithography?

DUV uses deep-ultraviolet light to pattern circuits on silicon wafers. It remains important for many chip types.

Why does the Netherlands oppose the proposal?

The government says export-control decisions should remain independent and opposes unilateral measures with extraterritorial effects.

Could the bill affect AI development?

Potentially, because chipmaking equipment affects the supply of processors used in data centres and AI systems. The actual impact depends on final rules.

Bottom line: The dispute is a test of allied coordination on technology security. It is not yet a settled export ban, and the outcome will depend on Congress and diplomacy.
