Anthropic IPO Selloff Tests Investor Confidence in AI Boom

The record public offering drew heavy demand, but shares fell sharply in early second-day trading as investors reassessed AI valuations and spending.

Published: November 21, 2026, 8:00 AM PKT · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Anthropic's record stock-market debut quickly turned into a test of investor patience. The company priced its initial public offering at $83 a share, but the stock opened well above that level and closed its first session at $97.17. During early trading the following day, shares fell to about $85, leaving some buyers who entered after the opening with losses.

The Anthropic IPO selloff came as global investors questioned the scale of spending on artificial intelligence and whether companies can convert infrastructure commitments into durable profits. The early price decline does not determine the company's long-term prospects, but it shows how quickly sentiment can change after a high-profile listing.

A record debut followed by a sharp reversal

Anthropic's first day on Nasdaq was historic. The company sold 980 million Class A shares at $83, raising about $81.3 billion in the public offering. Shares began trading at $112, well above the offering price, and closed at $97, according to Reuters. That was a strong first-day gain, but the opening surge also created a much higher entry price for investors buying on the exchange.

On Friday, Reuters reported that the stock dropped to about $85 by 12:20 p.m. Eastern, a decline of roughly 13% from the previous close. That intraday snapshot was not the final closing price. The movement showed how a newly listed stock can become volatile when early enthusiasm meets broader market pressure.

Why the IPO attracted such intense demand

Anthropic develops Claude, a family of AI models used by businesses, developers and consumers. Investors have been looking for direct exposure to companies building frontier AI rather than only buying shares in chipmakers, cloud providers or software firms. The offering's size and visibility made it a major test of public-market appetite.

Reuters reported that retail investors traded more than $750 million of Anthropic stock on its debut, according to Vanda Research. That was a record for a debut in the firm's data. Demand was strong, but less than 20% of shares were allocated to retail investors, leaving many buyers to trade in the secondary market.

The offering's scale raises the valuation question

Anthropic priced 980 million shares at $83, creating an $81.34 billion base offering. It also completed a concurrent private placement with technology companies, including AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC. The company announced on November 20 that the public offering and private placement had closed.

The closing announcement states that the full underwriters' option was exercised, increasing the Class A shares sold to 1.127 billion and gross IPO proceeds to about $93.5 billion. Including the $26 billion private placement, gross proceeds totalled approximately $119.5 billion before expenses. These are offering figures, not the company's market value.

AI valuations face a broader market test

The share decline occurred during a wider selloff in technology stocks. Reuters reported that investors were concerned about the huge spending required to train and deploy AI models and whether future revenue would justify the investment. Nvidia, Microsoft and Meta were among large technology companies under pressure during the period.

That backdrop matters because Anthropic's listing was expected to reinforce enthusiasm for AI. Instead, the second-day decline highlighted that investors may distinguish between a company's long-term technology potential and the price they are willing to pay for its shares. A high-profile IPO cannot insulate the sector from changing risk appetite.

Computing costs are central to the business model

Frontier AI requires powerful chips, large data centres, electricity and specialist engineering. Anthropic's expansion depends on access to cloud infrastructure and computing capacity. Those commitments can support future growth, but they also create substantial fixed costs and financing needs.

Investors will look at how efficiently the company converts computing investment into paid usage. Enterprise customers may pay for productivity, coding, research or customer-service tools, but the economics vary by workload. If model usage grows faster than costs, margins may improve; if inference costs remain high, growth may not immediately translate into profits.

The first-day price was not the IPO price

The $83 IPO price was available to allocated investors in the offering. Public-market buyers after the listing paid prices set by trading, which initially moved much higher. Reuters reported a first trade around $112 and a first-day close at $97. Those moves created different outcomes for investors depending on when they bought.

The Anthropic IPO selloff illustrates why the offering price and market price should not be confused. An IPO allocation may be difficult to obtain, while secondary-market trading can be volatile. The difference between the price paid and the value at a later moment is not a guaranteed profit or loss until the investor sells.

Retail investors were active but not fully allocated

Vanda Research's figures cited by Reuters indicate unusually high retail trading on the debut. However, the public allocation was limited, and investors who did not receive shares could only buy once trading began. That created a different risk profile from buying at the IPO price.

A fast-moving stock can attract social-media attention and short-term speculation. Investors should examine liquidity, price swings and their own risk tolerance rather than assume a high-profile listing will continue rising. The early reversal is a reminder that enthusiasm can be followed by profit-taking or reassessment.

The company's growth story still faces competition

Anthropic competes with OpenAI, Google and a growing group of open-source model developers. Businesses may choose among providers based on performance, price, security, reliability and integration with existing systems. The public listing gives investors a clearer way to track the company, but it does not guarantee market leadership.

Revenue growth, customer retention and product differentiation will be important indicators. The company also faces regulatory and safety questions as its models become more capable. Public shareholders will need to consider both the commercial opportunity and the risks that could affect deployment, reputation or operating costs.

What public-market investors can now monitor

As a listed company, Anthropic will face regular reporting requirements and greater scrutiny of financial performance. Investors can examine revenue growth, cash flow, operating expenses, capital commitments, customer concentration and management's outlook. The IPO provides access to public disclosures that were less frequent before listing.

Those metrics will be more useful than a single day's price movement. A company can experience volatile trading while its business expands, or a strong debut can be followed by weaker fundamentals. The Anthropic IPO selloff is a market signal, not a complete assessment of the company's value.

What the second-day drop means for the AI trade

The decline may encourage investors to demand clearer evidence that AI spending produces measurable returns. It could also influence how other AI companies approach public offerings, fundraising and valuation expectations. The market is weighing growth potential against infrastructure costs, competition and the timing of profits.

The immediate lesson is caution, not a verdict. Anthropic's listing remains a major event in technology finance, and its longer-term performance will depend on business results and market conditions. The early volatility shows that even a record offering can face a reality check when investors reassess price, risk and future earnings.
