Oil Prices Hold Near $93 as Pakistan Import Costs Face New Risks

Brent crude remained near $93 a barrel on November 18 as fresh US-Iran tensions and shipping risks offset evidence of higher US fuel stocks. Pakistan’s latest full-year import data show why sustained volatility matters, although a daily market move does not automatically determine local pump prices.

Published: November 18, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

1. What the oil market showed on November 18

International oil prices edged lower in early trading on November 18 after US inventory data showed a larger-than-expected build in crude and fuel stocks. Reuters reported that Brent was down 0.17% at $92.98 a barrel and US West Texas Intermediate had fallen 0.65% to $85.45 at 0149 GMT.

The decline was modest because traders were still weighing military escalation and the risk of further disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. The figures are a time-specific market snapshot, not a guaranteed closing price. Oil futures can move quickly as new inventory, security and diplomatic information arrives.

2. Why higher US inventories did not calm the market

The American Petroleum Institute data cited by Reuters showed US crude inventories rising by 5.28 million barrels in the week to November 13. Gasoline stocks increased by 5.12 million barrels, while distillate inventories gained 351,000 barrels. Higher inventories can suggest that near-term supply is more comfortable than traders expected.

But oil is traded globally, and regional supply risks can outweigh a single country’s stock report. Tanker attacks and uncertainty around the Gulf can make buyers worry about future deliveries even when inventories rise elsewhere. That tension helps explain why the market’s decline remained limited.

3. The Strait of Hormuz remains a key risk

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the wider Indian Ocean. Before the current conflict, around one-fifth of global oil and liquefied natural gas supplies passed through the waterway, making it one of the world’s most important maritime energy routes.

When ships face attacks, delays or higher insurance premiums, the cost of moving fuel can rise. Buyers may also seek alternative cargoes or longer routes. These changes can affect delivered prices even if benchmark crude futures do not rise sharply on a particular day.

4. Why Pakistan is exposed to global oil movements

Pakistan relies on imported crude and refined petroleum products to support transport, industry and other parts of the economy. Its exposure is not limited to the quoted price of a barrel: the dollar exchange rate, freight, insurance, refinery costs and the timing of cargo purchases also influence the local cost of energy.

That is why the Pakistan oil import bill is an important indicator for the external account. When the cost of imported energy rises, importers need more foreign currency for the same volume of fuel. If prices ease or domestic demand becomes more efficient, the pressure can lessen, although the effect may take time to appear.

5. What the latest official import data show

Pakistan Bureau of Statistics figures reported by the Associated Press of Pakistan show that the petroleum group import bill reached about $16.86 billion in fiscal year 2025–26, up 5.76% from the previous year. Petroleum products accounted for about $6.39 billion, while crude oil imports were about $7.17 billion. The Pakistan oil import bill was therefore already substantial before this latest November market move.

The same data showed liquefied natural gas imports falling to roughly $2.22 billion, down 36.10%, while liquefied petroleum gas imports rose 3.21% to about $1.09 billion. These are completed fiscal-year figures, not November 2026 monthly data. They provide a baseline for understanding Pakistan’s exposure rather than a live estimate of current spending.

6. How an oil shock can reach households and businesses

Higher imported fuel costs can feed into transport fares, freight charges, farm machinery, industrial production and the cost of moving food to markets. Businesses may absorb some of the increase, pass it on to customers or reduce other spending. The impact depends on how long prices remain high and how quickly costs move through supply chains.

Households can feel the effects through petrol and diesel prices, bus fares, delivery charges and the price of goods that depend on transport. A sustained rise would make the Pakistan oil import bill harder to manage and could increase pressure on household budgets. Electricity costs may also be affected when imported fuels are used in power generation. These channels are possible pressures, not proof that every price will rise immediately.

7. What could soften the impact

A fall in global crude prices would help, but it is not the only possible buffer. More efficient fuel use, reliable domestic energy production, diversified import routes and improved storage can reduce the economy’s exposure to sudden disruption. Pakistan’s growing use of solar power can also displace some fuel-fired electricity during daylight hours.

However, solar generation does not replace the diesel and petrol used by most road transport. The country therefore needs a broader approach that includes public transport, vehicle efficiency, better freight systems and dependable energy infrastructure. Reducing the Pakistan oil import bill over time will require several policies working together, not one quick fix.

8. Why a daily benchmark move is not a pump-price forecast

The movement in Brent or WTI futures is only one part of the price calculation faced by local fuel buyers. Import costs depend on the actual grades purchased, contract timing, freight, insurance, exchange rates and refining. Domestic taxes, levies, distribution margins and government pricing decisions also matter.

As a result, a small fall in oil futures on one morning does not guarantee an immediate reduction at Pakistani filling stations. Equally, a one-day rise does not prove that local prices must change by the same percentage. Consumers should rely on official domestic price notifications rather than extrapolating directly from international trading screens.

9. What businesses and policymakers should monitor

Importers and policymakers will need to watch the volume and timing of cargoes, the security of maritime routes, foreign-exchange liquidity and the direction of international product prices. The Pakistan oil import bill can also be influenced by how much fuel the country consumes, not simply by the price paid for each barrel.

For businesses, practical planning means stress-testing budgets against a range of fuel costs and avoiding assumptions that a temporary market dip will last. For the government, transparent stock information, predictable pricing decisions and careful management of foreign-currency requirements can help reduce uncertainty for consumers and industry.

10. The outlook is volatile, not certain

The November 18 market picture contains opposing forces. Higher US inventories and expectations of a better global supply balance can limit prices, while military incidents and threats to shipping can quickly restore a risk premium. The International Energy Agency’s November 13 Oil Market Report projected that global supply would average about 105 million barrels a day in the fourth quarter, roughly 1.7 million barrels above demand. The agency said continued attacks on shipping remain a downside risk to a sustained recovery.

For Pakistan, the central issue is resilience. The latest Pakistan oil import bill figures show the scale of the country’s exposure, while the latest market data show how quickly geopolitical events can influence benchmarks. A sustained improvement in shipping and diplomacy would help, but the path remains uncertain and should be assessed through verified data.

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to common questions about this story.

Why do rising global oil prices matter to Pakistan?

Pakistan imports crude oil and refined petroleum products. Higher international prices can increase the foreign currency needed to pay for energy imports and put pressure on the country’s trade balance and external account.

How much did Pakistan spend on petroleum imports in FY2025–26?

Pakistan Bureau of Statistics figures reported by the Associated Press of Pakistan put the petroleum group import bill at about $16.86 billion in FY2025–26, up 5.76% from the previous fiscal year.

Does a fall in Brent crude immediately reduce petrol prices in Pakistan?

No. Local fuel prices also depend on the exchange rate, import and freight costs, taxes, levies, refining and distribution costs, and the timing of government price reviews. A daily movement in Brent does not automatically change pump prices.

Why does the Strait of Hormuz affect Pakistan’s oil import costs?

The Strait of Hormuz is a key route for Gulf energy exports. Disruption or heightened security risks can increase tanker insurance, freight costs and delivery uncertainty for import-dependent countries, including Pakistan.

What factors determine Pakistan’s petrol and diesel prices?

International product and crude prices, the rupee-dollar exchange rate, freight and insurance, refinery and distribution margins, and government taxes or levies all influence domestic fuel prices.

Can higher oil prices increase inflation in Pakistan?

Sustained increases can raise transport, electricity generation, farming and manufacturing costs. Businesses may pass some of those costs to consumers, although the final effect depends on the duration of the oil shock and domestic policy decisions.
