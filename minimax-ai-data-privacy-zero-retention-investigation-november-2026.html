MiniMax AI Data Privacy Questions Grow After Probe

MiniMax says it disabled automated URL checks and found no evidence that full prompts or model responses were disclosed to outsiders.

Published November 26, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The MiniMax AI data privacy investigation has raised questions about how private information moves through artificial intelligence services. Security researcher HD Moore of Dragos told Reuters that confidential links submitted to Chinese AI company MiniMax were later accessed by unidentified parties, including in requests marked for no data retention. MiniMax says its automated security system opened some links to check for threats, but it found no evidence that full prompts or model responses were disclosed to third parties. The MiniMax AI data privacy case highlights a difficult issue for developers: a privacy label may not describe every company that handles a request.

What researchers say they found

Reuters reported on November 26 that Dragos chief research officer HD Moore identified MiniMax as the Chinese AI developer behind a previously unnamed investigation. Researchers said a secret link placed in a prompt later appeared in a Feishu chat and was opened by devices in Beijing and Shanghai. They also observed unusual internet activity from Russia, but said the evidence did not establish that the links were opened there. The findings concern specific URLs and attempts to retrieve private code. They do not prove that every MiniMax conversation was read or copied.

How the canary-link test worked

Dragos sent nearly two million requests containing unique web links and test login details to AI providers between July and November. The links were designed so researchers could detect later access. If a provider or another party opened a private test URL after a prompt had been sent, the team could trace that request. Reuters said the MiniMax pattern appeared when requests were forwarded to the company's cloud, not when other providers independently hosted the models. The method can reveal that a link was accessed, but it does not automatically show who accessed it or what other information they saw.

Why zero-data-retention labels matter

Zero Data Retention, often shortened to ZDR, is intended to prevent prompts and responses from being stored by a service. Developers may choose such settings when they send confidential code, customer records or internal documents to an AI model. The MiniMax AI data privacy investigation raised concerns because some links were accessed even when requests carried a no-retention label. That does not prove that every request was retained as a full conversation. It does show that URL data inside a prompt can be processed by security systems or intermediaries even when a customer expects limited storage.

MiniMax says its security system opened the links

MiniMax published a statement on November 24. It said its monitoring system extracted IP addresses and URLs from certain requests forwarded by OpenRouter and other inference providers. The system recorded those details in security logs and automated tools later probed some of the links to check for threats. MiniMax said the process was enabled by default on the relevant endpoints and included requests marked for zero data retention. The company said it disabled the mechanism on the identified path, stopped the activity for current business traffic and deleted the logged URLs.

What has and has not been disclosed

MiniMax said it found no evidence that full prompt content or model responses were disclosed to third parties beyond the URL activity it described. Reuters reported that the company did not address the later attempts to download private software-code collections or the appearance of one secret link in a Feishu chat. The researcher said it was unclear who initiated those attempts or whether any code was obtained. These gaps matter. The investigation documents access to test links and suspicious activity, but it does not establish that all submitted material was exposed or that a complete database was stolen.

Third-party routing can change where a prompt goes

Many developers do not connect directly to a model company. They use an API marketplace or an inference provider that forwards requests to another company. Each step can introduce a new data-handling policy, logging system or security tool. Atlas Cloud told Reuters that some information sent through its platform was potentially exposed after it reached MiniMax. The company said it stopped forwarding requests and warned affected customers to treat secrets as exposed. The MiniMax AI data privacy issue shows why customers need to know the full processing chain, not only the model name on the screen.

OpenRouter says it removed providers and tightened checks

OpenRouter said its own systems were not breached. In a November 22 update, it said it had removed three providers from its platform and taken 125 endpoints out of Zero Data Retention eligibility after broader investigations into provider data handling. The company said it notified potentially affected customers and was adapting the researchers' tests for ongoing checks. Those steps cover more than one provider incident. They show that AI routers are under pressure to verify the practices of the companies that actually run models and handle prompts.

Why businesses and developers should care

The MiniMax AI data privacy issue matters because AI tools are increasingly used to review source code, analyse contracts, summarise customer messages and help employees work with internal files. A private link or API key placed in a prompt can be sensitive even if the user never uploads a document directly. If an automated service opens that link later, it may reveal information about a private repository, a dashboard or a customer record. The risks are not limited to one country or one AI company. They apply whenever prompts move through providers whose security and retention practices are not fully understood.

Practical steps to reduce exposure

Developers should avoid putting passwords, API keys, access tokens or private URLs into ordinary prompts. When a credential may have appeared in an affected request, security teams should assess it and rotate it if necessary. Organisations should review which providers handle inference, whether requests are forwarded, what logs are retained and whether privacy settings apply to every hop. Sensitive workloads may need a direct enterprise agreement or a self-hosted model. Teams should also keep audit records and use synthetic test data when evaluating new AI services. The MiniMax AI data privacy debate makes those controls more urgent.

What to know next: five questions answered

The next stage in the MiniMax AI data privacy investigation will depend on independent verification of the company's changes and whether providers can demonstrate that requests follow the privacy settings selected by customers. Users should watch for further technical findings, provider notices and clearer explanations of how requests are routed.

Did researchers prove that all MiniMax conversations were leaked?

No. The evidence described specific URL access and other suspicious activity, not the disclosure of every conversation.

What did MiniMax admit?

It said its security system logged URLs and IP addresses from certain forwarded requests and automatically opened some links.

Did the activity include zero-retention requests?

MiniMax said the monitoring process also covered requests marked Zero Data Retention or no data collection.

What should affected users do?

Review prompts for secrets, rotate exposed credentials where appropriate and check provider routing and retention terms.

Was OpenRouter itself breached?

OpenRouter said the findings did not show a breach or unauthorised access to its own systems.

Conclusion: The case is a warning about the full chain behind an AI request. Clear retention controls, direct provider accountability and careful handling of credentials are essential if businesses are to trust AI services with sensitive work.
