Teamsters Press Congress for Chinese Car Ban

The Teamsters want Congress to turn existing connected-vehicle restrictions into a permanent law. The bill still needs votes in both chambers.

Published November 24, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The International Brotherhood of Teamsters is pressing Congress to pass a permanent law restricting Chinese vehicles in the United States. The union says connected cars could create risks for driver privacy, national security and domestic auto jobs. Its appeal arrives while lawmakers negotiate the Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026, a Senate bill that has cleared the Commerce Committee but has not become law. The debate is more complicated than a simple country-of-origin restriction. Existing Commerce Department rules already limit certain China-linked vehicle software and hardware, while the new bill would add ownership tests and compliance duties. A final Chinese car ban could affect automakers, suppliers and consumers well beyond the brands most often named in the debate.

Teamsters Put the Issue Back on Congress' Agenda

The Teamsters, a union representing about 1.3 million workers, urged lawmakers to move quickly before Congress adjourns for the year. Reuters reported that union president Sean O'Brien sent a letter arguing that Chinese automotive companies should not gain access to the US market. The union frames the issue around data security, industrial competition and American manufacturing jobs.

The request is political pressure, not a government decision. Senators and congressional aides were still discussing compromise language with House lawmakers, according to Reuters. The union's position reflects its members' interests and should be read as advocacy. Congress must weigh those arguments against competition, supply chains and vehicle prices.

What Congress Is Considering in S.4429

The central proposal is the Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026, introduced as Senate bill S.4429. The Senate Commerce, Science and Transportation Committee reported the bill favorably with a substitute amendment on September 22 and recommended that it pass. That is an important committee step, but it is not final passage by either chamber.

The measure would regulate connected vehicles and related software and hardware associated with foreign adversaries. The Senate report says the framework is intended to address surveillance, espionage and cyber risks. It also proposes compliance declarations, enforcement powers and review procedures. The final text matters because it may differ from political shorthand.

Ownership Thresholds Could Decide Which Companies Are Covered

The Senate report describes a 15 percent ownership or control threshold for connected-vehicle entities. It sets a separate 25 percent threshold for certain software and vehicle-connectivity hardware entities. The proposal also defines a foreign entity of concern through factors such as citizenship, residence, affiliation, ownership and control.

The bill identifies China, Russia, North Korea and Iran as covered countries. Its scope is therefore wider than the current argument about Chinese electric cars alone. Application would depend on the company, product, transaction and ownership structure. Lawmakers must explain how the thresholds work in complex global businesses.

Existing Commerce Rules Already Restrict Connected-Vehicle Technology

The United States does not start from a blank regulatory slate. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued a connected-vehicle rule in January 2025. It restricts certain transactions involving vehicle connectivity hardware and covered software with a sufficient nexus to China or Russia. The rule took effect on March 17, 2025.

The restrictions phase in by model year. Covered software prohibitions begin with model-year 2027 vehicles, while hardware restrictions generally begin with model-year 2030 vehicles. This is not a simple ban on every car manufactured in China. It targets specified technology and relationships regulators consider a national-security risk.

Why Connected Cars Raise National-Security Questions

Modern vehicles can collect location data, connect to mobile networks, receive software updates and communicate with cloud services. Some also use cameras, sensors and automated-driving systems. Those functions can improve safety and convenience, but they create data flows that regulators want to understand. The Senate report focuses on possible foreign access to connected-vehicle systems and information.

A risk assessment is not proof that every connected car is spying on its owner. The concern is whether a company or government could access sensitive data, disrupt a vehicle system or exploit a vulnerable supplier. The policy challenge is to define credible risks and apply rules that protect drivers without sweeping in unrelated products.

Mercedes-Benz and Volvo Show Why Ownership Is Complicated

Reuters reported that Chinese state-owned BAIC and Geely chairman Li Shufu each hold stakes close to 10 percent in Mercedes-Benz. US lawmakers asked the company to answer questions by December 1 about the combined stake and possible risks. The issue shows why ownership rules can reach beyond a car's assembly location.

Senator Bernie Moreno told Reuters that compromise language was intended to avoid banning Mercedes-Benz and would not bar Volvo, which is majority-owned by Geely Holding. Those comments describe the senator's account of negotiations, not a final legal interpretation. The bill's final language will determine how indirect ownership and control are assessed.

Auto Workers See a Jobs and Competitiveness Issue

The Teamsters argue that allowing Chinese automakers into the US market could put pressure on domestic producers and workers. Their concern reflects a broader debate over industrial policy, manufacturing capacity and the transition to electric vehicles. US tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles are already above 100 percent, according to Reuters, adding another layer to the trade dispute.

Automakers and suppliers operate across borders. A vehicle assembled in one country may contain batteries, software, sensors and components from several others. A new rule could encourage companies to move sourcing toward the United States or allied countries. It could also create compliance costs for suppliers mapping ownership and technology relationships.

Consumers Could Face Trade-Offs Between Security and Price

The Senate committee report includes a Congressional Budget Office assessment of possible economic effects. It says the bill could encourage sourcing and manufacturing shifts toward the United States and allied countries, but those changes may come at higher expense for consumers. Compliance and transition costs could affect manufacturers, importers, software developers and suppliers.

A tighter Chinese car ban could reduce the number of lower-priced models available if alternatives are more expensive or slower to reach the market. Supporters may argue that security protections justify those costs. Critics may ask whether narrower safeguards, audits or data controls could address risks with less disruption.

What Must Happen Before a Permanent Ban Becomes Law

The Senate committee report is a formal recommendation, not a final vote by Congress. The Senate and House must still agree on a text and pass it before it can go to the president. Reuters reported that Moreno hoped the House could vote as soon as the following week, but that was his expectation, not a confirmed floor schedule.

President Donald Trump has also said he would accept Chinese car companies building vehicles in the United States, Reuters reported in September. That comment adds uncertainty to the debate. The final text and any White House response will matter more than campaign-style headlines.

Conclusion: The Debate Is About Data, Ownership and Industrial Policy

The Teamsters' appeal has pushed connected vehicles back into the congressional spotlight. Existing Commerce rules already restrict certain China-linked software and hardware, while S.4429 would add ownership thresholds and a broader statutory framework. The proposal has cleared a Senate committee, but it has not become law.

A durable policy will need clear definitions, fair enforcement and transparent treatment of global supply chains. The central question is whether technology, ownership or control creates a measurable risk, and whether the response protects drivers without imposing unnecessary costs on workers and families. That is the test Congress faces as it considers the Chinese car ban.
