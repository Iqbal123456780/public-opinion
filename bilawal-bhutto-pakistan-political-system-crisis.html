Why Bilawal Bhutto Says Pakistan’s Political System Was Made to Collapse

At Karachi Press Club, the PPP chairman called for a political reset and raised questions about democratic representation, constitutional reform and the country’s governance debate.

Published: November 17, 2026 · Updated: November 18, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Pakistan’s political debate took a sharp turn on November 17, 2026, when Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari addressed the media at Karachi Press Club. The chairman of the Pakistan Peoples Party (PPP) argued that the country’s governance system had not simply collapsed. In his words, it had been “made to collapse”.

His remarks came during a wider debate about creating new administrative units and changing how the country is governed. Bilawal said Pakistan needed to understand the causes of its problems before choosing a solution. His speech placed the Pakistan political system, parliamentary trust and political cooperation at the centre of the discussion. This report explains the main points he raised and why they matter.

1. Why Bilawal Bhutto Says Pakistan’s Political System Was Made to Collapse

Bilawal was responding to comments by Interior Minister Mohsin Naqvi, who had spoken about the need for a governance reset and new administrative units. Bilawal said the debate focused too much on the system’s condition and not enough on why it had reached that point.

He linked the governance crisis to public concerns such as poverty, unemployment and inflation. His argument was that changing administrative boundaries alone would not solve these problems. The Pakistan governance crisis, in his view, requires political answers and a serious review of how institutions work together.

The difference between a reset and a structural change

A change in the number of provinces or administrative units would alter the country’s structure. A political reset, as Bilawal described it, would focus on trust, representation and decision-making. These are different proposals and should not be treated as interchangeable.

2. Bilawal Bhutto Calls for a Political Reset to Restore Democracy in Pakistan

Bilawal said a reset was necessary but insisted that it must be political. He argued that elected representatives should take responsibility for resolving national problems. He also said political leaders needed to correct past mistakes rather than repeat them.

He raised concerns about public confidence in state institutions. Restoring that confidence, he suggested, requires dialogue and meaningful parliamentary participation. The idea of a political reset in Pakistan therefore goes beyond changing officeholders. It involves rebuilding trust between political parties and the institutions that serve the public.

Why political consensus matters

Political consensus does not mean every party must agree on every policy. It means major national decisions should include debate, consultation and respect for constitutional procedures. Without that process, reforms can deepen divisions instead of solving them.

3. What Bilawal Said About PTI’s Reserved Seats and Election Symbol

Bilawal criticised the treatment of the opposition over reserved parliamentary seats and party identity. He described the reserved-seats dispute as an injustice and said returning seats to the opposition could help rebuild trust. He also referred to the importance of a political party retaining its election symbol.

The dispute has been part of a wider legal and political conflict involving the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI). The Supreme Court had ruled on the reserved-seats issue before a constitutional bench later overturned that decision while hearing review petitions. Bilawal argued that correcting what he sees as unfair treatment could serve as a confidence-building step.

Why reserved seats and party symbols matter

Reserved seats are designed to provide representation for groups such as women and non-Muslims in legislatures. Party symbols also help voters identify candidates and political parties on ballot papers. Disputes over either issue can affect how citizens view the fairness of elections.

4. Why Bilawal Bhutto Called Imran Khan a Political Prisoner

Bilawal renewed his call to end political victimisation and said Pakistan should not have political prisoners. When asked directly about PTI founder Imran Khan, he answered that he considered him a political prisoner. This was Bilawal’s political assessment during the press interaction.

He also acknowledged that the PTI had made mistakes. His broader point was that political revenge should not become a permanent feature of national politics. A stable parliamentary system needs opposition parties to participate and challenge government decisions without fear of politically motivated punishment.

What this could mean for parliamentary politics

Reducing political hostility could make negotiations easier on legislation, budgets and national security. However, legal cases must still follow due process. Political leaders’ statements do not replace court decisions or determine the outcome of individual cases.

5. Pakistan’s New Provinces Debate: Why Bilawal Opposes a Referendum

The proposal to create new provinces has become a major part of the national governance debate. Bilawal said the PPP would oppose any unconstitutional move and rejected the idea of using a referendum as a shortcut. He argued that constitutional procedures should guide any change to provincial boundaries.

He also spoke against what he described as a forced division of Sindh. He said the PPP would welcome debate and consensus but would oppose unconstitutional steps. The issue is especially sensitive because provincial identity, resources and political representation are involved. The debate over new provinces in Pakistan therefore requires consultation with elected representatives and affected communities.

Why the Sindh division debate is sensitive

Supporters of new administrative units often argue that smaller provinces could improve service delivery. Opponents worry about political pressure, resource distribution and local identity. Any proposal needs transparent public discussion and compliance with the Constitution rather than assumptions about what residents want.

6. Bilawal Questions the Federal Government’s Role in Pakistan’s Governance Crisis

Bilawal asked the federal government to explain its position on the proposed governance reset. He said the issue had been discussed publicly without, in his account, proper debate in the cabinet or Parliament. He also questioned whether ministers were speaking with a clear government mandate.

He referred to Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and said the federal leadership should clarify its approach. Bilawal also raised the distribution of financial resources between the Centre and Sindh. These comments connect the Pakistan federal government debate with questions about provincial autonomy and public spending.

Federalism and provincial responsibilities

Federalism divides powers and responsibilities between national and provincial governments. Disagreements over money, services and authority can create tension. Clear rules, transparent budgets and regular consultation can help governments resolve disputes without weakening the federation.

7. Pakistan’s 27th Constitutional Amendment: Why Bilawal Demands Political Consensus

Bilawal criticised the way the 27th Constitutional Amendment was passed, saying it lacked political consensus and was approved through a brute majority. He contrasted it with the 26th Amendment, which he described as having been passed through a bilateral agreement after opposition input was secured through Maulana Fazlur Rehman.

He questioned whether the new constitutional court had the confidence of lawyers and judges. He said he was not calling for the changes to be revoked, but wanted greater political consensus and engagement with the opposition on judicial reforms. Constitutional changes can shape institutions for years, so the process used to pass them matters alongside their legal provisions.

Why parliamentary consensus matters for constitutional reform

Constitutional amendments can affect the balance of power between institutions. Broad consultation can make reforms more legitimate and durable. The exact legal effect of an amendment should be assessed through the constitutional text and court rulings, not political statements alone.

8. Bilawal Defends Maulana Fazlur Rehman’s Right to Criticise the Government

Bilawal defended the right of opposition leaders to criticise the government. He specifically referred to Maulana Fazlur Rehman, head of Jamiat Ulema-e-Islam-Fazl (JUI-F), amid recent disagreements between the party and federal ministers. He also criticised the use of pressure against opposition figures and urged the Centre to engage with Fazlur Rehman.

Bilawal said criticism is a normal part of parliamentary democracy. He argued that questioning an opposition member’s patriotism because of political disagreement harms Parliament and public trust. He also discussed governor’s rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, saying it should not remain in place indefinitely and that talks with the opposition should resume.

The opposition’s role in a democracy

An effective opposition examines government decisions, raises concerns and offers alternatives. It can also hold ministers accountable through parliamentary debate. Democratic institutions work best when criticism is answered with evidence and discussion rather than personal attacks.

9. How Political Instability Is Affecting Pakistan’s Democracy and Economy

Political instability can make it harder for governments to plan long-term policies. Businesses may delay investment when rules appear uncertain. Public services can also suffer when political disputes prevent institutions from focusing on education, health, jobs and infrastructure.

Bilawal connected the need for a political reset with issues such as inflation and unemployment. Those problems have many causes, including economic conditions and policy choices. Still, predictable institutions and cooperation among political parties can help create a more stable environment for reform and investment.

What should happen next?

The next steps depend on whether political leaders can move from speeches to practical negotiations. Parliament, provincial assemblies and relevant institutions all have roles to play. Any reform should be transparent, constitutional and focused on improving people’s lives.

Conclusion: Pakistan Needs Dialogue Before Another Political Reset

Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari’s speech brought several sensitive issues back into the national debate: political representation, the treatment of opposition parties, new provinces and constitutional reform. His central argument was that Pakistan must address the causes of its governance problems instead of relying on structural changes alone.

Whether political parties agree with his proposals or not, the questions he raised require serious public discussion. A stronger Pakistan political system depends on constitutional rules, credible institutions and space for peaceful disagreement. The real test will be whether leaders can turn calls for consensus into practical action.
