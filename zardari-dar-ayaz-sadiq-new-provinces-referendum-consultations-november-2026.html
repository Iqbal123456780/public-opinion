Zardari Consults Dar on New Provinces Referendum

President Zardari met Ishaq Dar and Ayaz Sadiq as Pakistan's new provinces referendum debate intensifies, with coalition partners seeking constitutional consensus.

Published November 22, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

President Asif Ali Zardari met Deputy Prime Minister Ishaq Dar and National Assembly Speaker Ayaz Sadiq at Aiwan-e-Sadr as political leaders continue debating the creation of new provinces. The meeting followed consultations involving Dar, Sadiq and Balochistan Chief Minister Sarfraz Bugti, according to local reporting.

The new provinces referendum debate has exposed differences within the governing coalition over how administrative reform should proceed. The latest meetings produced no publicly announced decision, but they show senior officials are discussing the issue while parties defend their constitutional positions.

A high-level meeting brings the debate back into focus

The Nation reported that Zardari, Dar and Sadiq met on Saturday night to review the political situation, including the proposal for a referendum on creating new provinces. Former prime minister Raja Pervez Ashraf and Senator Sherry Rehman were also reported to have attended. The meeting took place at Aiwan-e-Sadr in Islamabad.

The timing matters because the debate has moved from individual statements into consultations among coalition leaders and parliamentary figures. The new provinces referendum question is politically sensitive, particularly where provincial identity and boundaries are involved. No formal agreement or timetable was announced in the reporting reviewed.

No formal decision has been announced

Reports describe discussions rather than a policy decision. The Presidency has not publicly released a detailed outcome setting out a referendum date, proposed province boundaries or a draft constitutional amendment. The absence of a formal announcement means the meeting should not be treated as approval of a new province.

Political consultations can help narrow differences, but a final proposal would need to identify what administrative changes are being considered and which institutions would be responsible. Until officials publish a proposal, questions about the scope and legal process remain open.

Dar and Sadiq also consulted Bugti

The Nation said Dar and Speaker Sadiq had earlier met Balochistan Chief Minister Sarfraz Bugti at the Foreign Office. That meeting also covered the political situation and matters connected with proposed new provinces. The consultations indicate that federal and provincial leaders are being brought into the discussion.

Bugti's participation is notable because any constitutional restructuring would require the involvement of affected provincial institutions. However, the public reports do not provide a detailed record of his position or show that the participants agreed on a specific administrative map.

Coalition partners remain divided

The Pakistan Peoples Party has resisted proposals that could divide Sindh, while some federal ministers have argued that smaller administrative units could improve service delivery. The debate has intensified after PPP Chairman Bilawal Bhutto-Zardari warned against what he described as forced division of provinces.

The disagreement creates a challenge for the coalition. Both sides may support better governance in principle, but they differ on whether new provinces, a referendum or stronger local government would be the best route. The new provinces referendum issue could therefore become a test of coalition coordination.

Government ministers support asking the public

Information Minister Attaullah Tarar has described a referendum as a constitutional way to gauge public opinion on administrative reforms and new provinces. Federal Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry also said a referendum would happen, according to Dawn's reporting on November 20.

Supporters argue that a public vote could give political leaders a clearer mandate. Critics counter that a referendum cannot replace constitutional procedures or provincial consent. The new provinces referendum proposal remains a political argument, not a scheduled national vote.

Article 48 sets conditions for a referendum

Article 48(6) of Pakistan's Constitution allows the prime minister to refer a matter of national importance to a joint sitting of Parliament for approval before it is put to a yes-or-no referendum. The provision does not authorize the prime minister to call a vote unilaterally.

Article 48(7) allows Parliament to establish the procedure for holding a referendum and consolidating its results. The constitutional text is available through the Senate's Constitution of Pakistan document. Legal questions would need to be addressed before any ballot.

Changing provincial boundaries needs constitutional amendment

A referendum alone cannot create a province or change provincial boundaries. Article 239 requires a constitutional amendment to pass by at least two-thirds of the total membership of each parliamentary house. If a bill changes a province's limits, that province's assembly must also approve it by two-thirds.

The provincial requirement is central to the debate. It means political leaders would need to build support beyond the federal government, especially among lawmakers in the province whose boundaries could change. This legal threshold makes consensus a practical requirement, not simply a political preference.

Sindh remains a sensitive part of the discussion

The PPP has repeatedly opposed dividing Sindh, and the Sindh Assembly previously passed a resolution rejecting further division of the province. Party leaders argue that provincial identity, elected representation and constitutional safeguards should not be weakened by unilateral proposals.

Supporters of smaller units often point to distance from provincial capitals, administrative access and uneven development. The new provinces referendum debate in Sindh is therefore about more than maps: it touches language, identity, resources, representation and the distribution of authority.

Administrative reform has alternatives

Creating provinces is not the only way to improve public services. Local governments, stronger district administration, transparent budgets and clearer responsibilities can address some complaints about access to services. Pakistan's Constitution also provides for local government under Article 140-A, with provincial legislation shaping the system.

A careful reform discussion would compare the costs of new provincial institutions with the benefits of closer administration. It would also consider revenue-sharing, police and civil-service structures, health and education budgets, and how residents would access services during a transition.

What happens next will depend on consultation

The immediate next step appears to be continued political discussion rather than a formal referendum process. Parties may seek a joint position, ask Parliament to examine the constitutional questions or redirect attention toward local-government reforms. None of those paths has been formally announced as the outcome of the latest meeting.

The new provinces referendum debate will remain unsettled until leaders publish a proposal and explain its legal route. Readers should distinguish reported consultations, political statements and constitutional action. A concrete timetable, parliamentary motion or provincial resolution would provide clearer evidence of progress.

Frequently asked questions

Did Zardari, Dar and Ayaz Sadiq agree to create new provinces?

No agreement was publicly announced. Reports say they discussed the political situation and the referendum proposal.

No date or formal referendum plan was announced in the reporting reviewed.

Can the prime minister call a referendum alone?

Article 48(6) requires approval at a joint sitting of Parliament before a national-importance referendum can be held.

Can a referendum create a province?

No. Changing provincial boundaries requires a constitutional amendment and approval by the affected provincial assembly.

Why does Sindh oppose division?

PPP leaders and the Sindh Assembly have opposed division, citing provincial unity, constitutional consent and political concerns.
