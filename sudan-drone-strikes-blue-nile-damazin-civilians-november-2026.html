Sudan Drone Strikes Kill 19 Civilians in Blue Nile State

By Public Opinion News Editorial Team · Published November 25, 2026

Sudan drone strikes in the southeastern Blue Nile state killed 19 civilians and wounded 20 others, according to the rights group Emergency Lawyers and residents who spoke to Reuters. The attacks struck residential neighborhoods in Damazin and facilities near Roseires, including infrastructure linked to the local power supply. Emergency Lawyers blamed the Rapid Support Forces, but the group did not immediately respond to Reuters' request for comment. Witnesses confirmed that drone strikes occurred; they did not independently establish who launched them. The incident adds to the danger faced by civilians as Sudan's war enters another phase.

What happened in Damazin

Emergency Lawyers said Sudan drone strikes hit three residential neighborhoods in Damazin, the capital of Blue Nile state, on Tuesday night. The group reported 19 deaths, including six women, and 15 injuries in the city. It said the victims came from three families. Reuters spoke with residents who confirmed that drone strikes had taken place. The casualty figures remain attributed to the rights group because independent access to the affected neighborhoods is limited. The conflict has made rapid verification difficult, especially when communications, medical services and safe travel are disrupted.

Roseires attacks raised concern about power facilities

Five other civilians were wounded in Roseires, according to Emergency Lawyers. The group said drones targeted the dam and power facilities and warned that further damage could disrupt electricity supplies across Blue Nile. That warning matters because electricity supports hospitals, water pumps, communications and basic public services. The available reporting does not establish the extent of damage to the dam itself. Readers should distinguish the reported targeting of infrastructure from a confirmed failure of the dam or the national grid.

Who was blamed, and what is confirmed

Emergency Lawyers attributed the Sudan drone strikes to Sudan's paramilitary Rapid Support Forces. Reuters reported that the RSF did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Witnesses confirmed drone activity, but that does not by itself prove who controlled the aircraft or selected the targets. Another local rights group gave a different injury estimate, illustrating how difficult casualty verification remains. The careful description is that a rights group blamed the RSF, residents confirmed strikes, and independent verification of every detail was not available at publication.

Why Blue Nile state matters

Blue Nile sits in southeastern Sudan along the borders with Ethiopia and South Sudan. The state contains important transport routes, agricultural areas and power infrastructure. Damazin and Roseires have also received people fleeing fighting elsewhere in the region, including Bau and Geissan, according to Emergency Lawyers. When Sudan drone strikes reach towns that are already hosting displaced families, the impact can extend beyond the immediate casualty count. Local services face more pressure, and residents may have fewer safe places to move.

Drone warfare has changed the conflict

The war between Sudan's armed forces and the RSF began in April 2023. Both sides have increasingly used drones to strike targets far from traditional front lines. A Reuters report citing the U.N. human rights office said 2,230 civilian deaths were confirmed between January and June 2026, with more than 1,630 attributed to drone strikes. The U.N. warned that the real toll could be higher. Those figures cover a wider period and region; they should not be confused with the casualties reported in Damazin and Roseires.

Civilians face a wider humanitarian emergency

Sudan's conflict has displaced millions and damaged health, education, water and electricity services. Humanitarian agencies have repeatedly warned that families face shortages of food, shelter and medical care. The latest Sudan drone strikes add pressure to communities that may already be supporting people who fled nearby fighting. A damaged power network can affect clinics and water systems well beyond the strike site. A civilian casualty count is only one measure of the impact. Displacement, interrupted services and fear of further attacks can deepen the emergency over time.

The risk to infrastructure goes beyond one town

The reported targeting of facilities near Roseires matters because power networks are connected systems. Damage at a single location can affect other communities if there are few backup routes or spare parts. It is not yet clear whether the reported strikes caused lasting damage or a sustained electricity outage. Emergency Lawyers warned about the risk, not a confirmed nationwide blackout. Engineers and independent monitors would need access to assess the physical condition of the facilities and the scale of any disruption.

Regional tensions complicate the fighting

Blue Nile's location near Ethiopia and South Sudan gives the fighting a regional dimension. The RSF said in August that it had captured Geissan, which lies near the Ethiopian border. Sudan's government and the RSF have made competing claims about territorial control and external support. Those claims require careful attribution. A battlefield statement does not independently prove control of a town, and an accusation of foreign backing is not evidence by itself. The latest Sudan drone strikes underline why border monitoring and humanitarian access matter.

What international law requires

International humanitarian law requires parties to distinguish civilians from combatants and to take precautions to avoid civilian harm. Attacks on civilian homes and infrastructure that supports essential services raise serious legal questions. A definitive legal assessment would require evidence about the target, the information available to the attacker, expected civilian harm and the military objective claimed. The available reports do not provide that full record. Independent investigations, access to survivors and preservation of evidence will be important if accountability is pursued.

What to watch next: verification and humanitarian access

The next questions are whether investigators can verify the casualty figures, whether the Roseires facilities remain operational and whether displaced families receive safe access to shelter and medical care. Sudan drone strikes have become a major feature of the conflict, but each incident still needs its own evidence trail.
