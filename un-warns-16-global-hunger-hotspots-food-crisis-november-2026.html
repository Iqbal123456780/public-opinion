UN Warns 16 Hunger Hotspots Face Worsening Crisis

A new FAO-WFP warning identifies 16 areas where acute food insecurity is expected to worsen between December 2026 and May 2027.

Published November 27, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The latest warning on global hunger hotspots points to a widening food-security emergency across Africa, the Middle East and Central America. The Food and Agriculture Organization and the World Food Programme said on November 27 that acute food insecurity is expected to worsen in 16 hotspots between December 2026 and May 2027. Conflict remains the main driver in many places, while high food and transport costs, shrinking aid budgets and a developing El Niño could deepen the crisis. The report is an early-warning assessment, not a declaration that famine is already occurring in every listed area.

Why the number of hunger hotspots has risen

The FAO and WFP list 16 hotspots in their new outlook, compared with 13 in the report released in June. Chad, Uganda, parts of southern Africa and the Central American Dry Corridor have been added, while Lebanon was removed from the list. The agencies say the new entries reflect worsening risks rather than a single event or one common cause.

The global hunger hotspots will be judged by the practical steps that follow, not by the language of the announcement alone.

Five places face the gravest concern

The agencies identify Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, northern Nigeria and Yemen as the five most critical hotspots in terms of severity and scale. These countries face different combinations of conflict, displacement, economic pressure and weather shocks. In each case, families may be unable to reach markets, earn income, grow food or receive enough humanitarian support.

El Niño adds a weather risk

The report warns that a developing El Niño could bring hotter and drier conditions to southern Africa and parts of Central America, while increasing the chance of destructive flooding in eastern Africa. The effect will not be identical in every country. Some areas may face failed rains and poor harvests, while others could experience heavy rainfall, damaged roads and disrupted planting.

Conflict remains the biggest driver

Conflict and violence are driving hunger in 12 of the 16 hotspots, according to the agencies. Fighting can force families from their homes, prevent farmers from reaching fields and block the roads used to move food. It can also destroy local markets, raise transport costs and make it dangerous for humanitarian teams to deliver assistance.

Aid cuts weaken the early-warning response

FAO official Conor Elliott told Reuters that assistance to the five most threatened countries fell by about two-thirds between 2022 and 2025. The reduction affects more than food parcels. Humanitarian agencies need funding to monitor prices, assess nutrition, support farmers and maintain access routes. When that work is cut, decision-makers can lose the information needed to target aid.

Southern Africa and Central America are newly exposed

Southern Africa is one of the new additions, with Eswatini, Malawi, Mozambique, Zambia and Zimbabwe included in the regional hotspot. The agencies warn that hotter, drier conditions could harm planting and harvests. Zimbabwe, Malawi and Mozambique are among the countries where millions may face high levels of acute food insecurity in the months ahead.

What the report says about Gaza and other crises

Afghanistan, Haiti, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Gaza remain areas of very high concern. Gaza was moved down from the highest level after improvements in food security following the October 2025 ceasefire, but the agencies say conditions remain severe and fragile. A lower warning level does not mean the humanitarian crisis has ended.

How early action can prevent a deeper emergency

Early action includes helping farmers obtain seeds, tools, livestock treatment and access to water before a planting window closes. It can also mean cash support for households, nutrition services for children and pregnant women, and repairs to roads or storage facilities. The right response depends on the local cause of the crisis and the people most at risk.

Humanitarian agencies also need safe access. A food shipment cannot help if it cannot reach a community, and a crop programme cannot succeed if farmers cannot travel to their land. The global hunger hotspots warning calls for governments and donors to act early, coordinate across agencies and protect the people who deliver assistance.

Why food insecurity affects economies and stability

Food shortages can quickly spread beyond households. When crops fail, rural incomes fall and local businesses lose customers. Higher food prices can push families to spend less on health, education and transport. Governments may face pressure to expand subsidies or emergency imports at a time when public budgets are already strained.

Displacement can also affect neighbouring countries. Families who leave areas of conflict or drought need shelter, services and jobs. Host communities may face extra pressure on schools, clinics and water systems. The global hunger hotspots outlook therefore matters to development planners as well as humanitarian agencies, because food security is linked to jobs, migration and social stability.

What to watch between now and May 2027

The next six months will test whether forecasts turn into stronger harvests or deeper shortages. Governments will watch rainfall, crop conditions, food prices and displacement. Aid groups will monitor access to conflict areas and whether funding is arriving before emergency needs peak. The next report will also help show whether the current warnings were acted on in time.

The FAO and WFP have identified risks, not fixed outcomes. Better rains, safer access and early assistance could reduce pressure in some places. Continued fighting, severe weather or further aid cuts could make conditions worse. The global hunger hotspots report is most useful when it prompts action before families face the hardest choices.

Five questions readers are asking

Which countries face the gravest risk?

Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, northern Nigeria and Yemen are the five areas of highest concern.

Does a hotspot mean famine has been declared?

No. It is an early-warning category for expected deterioration in acute food insecurity.

What is El Niño expected to change?

It could worsen drought and heat in southern Africa and Central America while increasing flood risks in eastern Africa.

Conclusion: The new warning gives governments and donors a clear window to act before the next harvest cycle. Conflict, climate shocks and funding cuts are reinforcing one another, but early support can still protect lives and livelihoods. The next six months will show whether the warning leads to timely action.
