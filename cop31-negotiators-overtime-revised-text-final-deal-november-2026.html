COP31 Talks Overtime as Final Deal Remains Uncertain

COP31 talks in Antalya enter another overtime stretch as a revised climate package faces scrutiny over fossil fuels, finance and adaptation.

Published November 22, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Negotiators remained uncertain as talks worked beyond the conference's scheduled finish in Antalya, Türkiye. A revised package presented by the summit presidency was described as a possible basis for consensus, but the text had not been publicly released in the latest reporting reviewed.

Reuters and the Associated Press reported that talks were still addressing fossil-fuel language, climate finance and the role of carbon capture. As of this briefing, no adopted final decision had been confirmed. The COP31 climate deal will depend on whether nearly 200 delegations can accept the compromise.

Negotiations continue after the scheduled close

COP31 was scheduled to end on November 20, but negotiators continued into the weekend as disagreements persisted. Overtime is common at UN climate conferences, yet the extended talks show how difficult it remains to balance national energy policies, climate targets and financial commitments.

The package has become a test of diplomacy because the conference works through consensus. Governments are negotiating a package rather than voting on separate proposals, so unresolved wording on one issue can delay the entire decision.

The presidency presented a revised package

Turkish environment minister and COP president Murat Kurum said early Sunday that he had a text he believed all parties could accept. Reuters reported that the package followed consultations involving Türkiye, Australia, and delegations from nearly 200 countries.

The presidency's confidence does not mean the text has been approved. Delegates may request changes, object to provisions or seek clarification. The COP31 climate deal remains a proposal until the conference adopts its final decision.

The text was not publicly available at the latest cutoff

The Associated Press reported that the new proposal had not been made public early Sunday and that it was unclear whether all negotiators had received the wording. Reuters reported an objection deadline of 03:00 GMT and a session scheduled for 06:00 GMT to begin approval.

At 08:22 Pakistan time, the sources reviewed had not confirmed an adopted text. Readers should treat the package as a negotiating proposal, not an agreement. A published final decision would provide the clearest record of what countries accepted.

Fossil fuels remain the most divisive issue

An earlier draft drew criticism because it omitted explicit language about transitioning away from coal, oil and gas. Some governments and campaigners wanted the final text to build on commitments made at previous climate summits and outline a clearer pathway for reducing fossil-fuel dependence.

Others resisted language that could be interpreted as imposing a uniform timetable on national energy systems. The COP31 climate deal is therefore caught between the scientific case for rapid emissions cuts and governments' concerns about energy security, economic costs and domestic politics.

Climate finance is another unresolved pressure point

Developing countries have argued that adaptation and resilience require more predictable support. Funding is needed for flood protection, drought planning, heat response, public infrastructure and recovery after climate-related disasters. Negotiators have debated how wealthy countries should contribute and how funds should reach vulnerable communities.

Finance discussions involve grants, loans, private investment and reporting rules. Governments differ over what counts as support and how to share responsibility. The COP31 climate deal could face criticism if it leaves commitments vague or fails to establish a credible route for delivering money.

Electrification offers a possible area of compromise

The draft package has discussed increasing the share of final energy use supplied by electricity. Electrification can reduce emissions in transport, buildings and industry, but the climate benefit depends on the power mix. Renewable generation, storage and grid investment determine whether demand shifts away from fossil fuels.

Countries disagree over whether targets should explicitly prioritize clean electricity or leave room for different technologies. The issue also connects climate policy with industrial competition, energy access and growing electricity demand from data centres and electric vehicles.

Carbon capture divides governments and campaigners

Carbon capture and storage has appeared in negotiations as a tool for sectors that are difficult to decarbonize. Supporters say it may reduce emissions from cement, steel and some energy processes. Critics warn that it can be expensive, slow to scale and used to justify continued fossil-fuel production.

The technology's role in the final package is likely to depend on how it is described and whether it complements emissions reductions rather than replaces them. A climate decision that emphasizes carbon removal without a clear reduction pathway could face strong scrutiny.

The 1.5-degree goal is increasingly difficult to reach

The UN has warned that current policies and pledges put the world on a path to exceed the Paris Agreement's 1.5-degree warming threshold. That limit is not a switch between safe and unsafe conditions, but crossing it increases risks from extreme heat, heavy rainfall, drought and ecosystem damage.

Negotiators face pressure to connect scientific warnings with measurable action. The COP31 climate deal could acknowledge the risk, but an acknowledgment alone would not reduce emissions. Implementation depends on national plans, investment and the pace of energy-system change.

Consensus gives every delegation leverage

UN climate decisions are generally adopted by consensus, allowing countries to object to a package they consider unacceptable. That rule encourages broad agreement but can also produce diluted language or extended negotiations when interests conflict.

Vulnerable states want stronger commitments on adaptation and emissions, while fossil-fuel producers and energy-dependent economies seek flexibility. The presidency must balance those positions without losing support from the countries most exposed to climate impacts.

What to watch in the approval session

The next key evidence will be the public text, any objections raised by delegations and the outcome of the plenary session. If the package is adopted, its language on fossil fuels, finance, electrification and adaptation will determine how governments interpret the decision.

If objections remain, negotiators may continue revising the package or defer elements to later meetings. The COP31 climate deal should be judged against the final adopted text, not optimistic statements made while negotiations are still underway.
