AI-Assisted Riemann Proof Passes Formal Checks

A formal proof developed with Claude received strong verification from the Lean and Mathlib communities, raising new questions about AI and mathematical research.

Published November 23, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

A mathematician’s Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis has received an unusually strong endorsement from the communities that maintain the Lean proof assistant and its mathematics library. Levent Alpöge released a large formalization on November 20, and the Lean and Mathlib communities said the statement and proof were correct the following day. The Riemann hypothesis AI proof story is significant because formal checking can test every logical step in a machine-readable argument. It is not the same as a human-readable paper, journal publication or a Millennium Prize decision. Those processes remain separate, and the mathematical community will continue examining what the proof means.

What was announced

Levent Alpöge said he had worked with Claude to develop a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, a problem proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. He released the work as a Lean library rather than a conventional paper. The Lean community statement describes the release as about 17 million lines of code.

This was Alpöge’s research claim, not a product announcement by Anthropic. Independent communities examined the formalization and reported that it checks out.

Why the Riemann hypothesis matters

The hypothesis concerns the zeros of the Riemann zeta function and the distribution of prime numbers. It predicts that every non-trivial zero lies on a particular vertical line in the complex plane. Prime numbers appear irregularly, but the hypothesis would give mathematicians a powerful description of how closely their distribution follows expected patterns.

It is one of seven Millennium Prize Problems, each with a $1 million award under Clay’s rules. A proof would be a major result in number theory.

Lean turns mathematical arguments into code

Lean is a proof assistant: a programming environment in which mathematical statements and proofs are written in a formal language. Its kernel checks whether the proof term follows from the definitions and axioms used. That process can catch logical gaps that might be difficult to notice in a long handwritten argument.

Formalization requires precise definitions and lemmas. The software checks each step. AI may help generate code, but the checker—not the model’s confidence—determines whether the encoded proof passes.

What the Lean and Mathlib communities verified

The joint statement said the communities had full confidence in Alpöge’s proof and certified that both the statement and proof were correct. Their review included checking that the formal statement matched Mathlib’s definition of the Riemann hypothesis and that the proof met the community’s verification criteria.

This matters because a machine can prove a theorem that is not the theorem a researcher intended. Formal verification must check both the derivation and the statement. The community’s certification addresses that formal question, not every aspect of human mathematical understanding or institutional recognition.

Independent checks add confidence

MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland published a verification log describing several checks on the released code. The log reported that the modules compiled, that proof dependencies used Lean’s standard axioms, and that independent checking tools accepted the exported proof. The Lean and Mathlib statement also described verification work by the community.

These checks can be rerun by researchers and reduce the risk of coding errors. Verification logs should still distinguish completed checks from any long-running tests.

A formal proof is not yet a readable explanation

The released archive is extremely large and was not accompanied by a complete conventional mathematical exposition. A formal proof can be correct while its underlying ideas remain difficult for people to understand. Mathematicians will want a narrative showing the strategy, the key lemmas and why the argument works.

A human explanation serves a different purpose: it helps specialists identify connections, assess the method and teach the result. Formal verification has not ended the discussion about mathematical insight.

The role of AI—and the limits of the claim

Alpöge credited Claude with substantial work near the end of the process. The model’s contribution raises questions about how mathematicians should describe authorship, responsibility and reproducibility when AI systems help build formal arguments. The researcher remains responsible for the claim and for explaining the result.

AI can explore strategies and assist with formalization, but it can also produce errors or difficult-to-inspect code. A proof assistant checks the formal result, not the model’s creativity or efficiency.

Why Clay’s prize process remains separate

Clay’s rules require a proposed solution to be published in a qualifying outlet, remain published for at least two years and receive general acceptance from the mathematical community. The Institute still lists the Riemann hypothesis as unsolved for prize purposes. The formal verification announcement does not award the prize or change those rules.

That distinction avoids confusing a proof-checking milestone with a formal institutional decision. The Riemann hypothesis AI proof can be a major mathematical development while publication, review and broader acceptance continue. The prize process is designed to give the community time to assess a claimed solution.

What formal verification could change in research

Formal tools may become more common in mathematics as proof libraries grow and AI systems become better at translating informal ideas into code. Researchers could use them to check difficult arguments, verify lemmas and share reproducible proof artifacts. That could make some research more reliable and reduce time spent checking routine steps.

But formalization takes expertise and computing resources. A successful proof may depend on a large library of prior definitions and results. The challenge for the field is to make the tools accessible, transparent and sustainable, rather than relying on a few specialists or proprietary systems.

The next milestones

Researchers will continue checking the archive and preparing a readable account. Journal publication and discussion among number theorists would help the result enter the broader literature.

Readers should follow primary updates from the Lean community, MIT’s verification log and the mathematician’s own announcement. Headlines may simplify the status, so it is useful to ask what exactly has been checked and which milestones remain. The Riemann hypothesis AI proof story is a case study in separating evidence, interpretation and recognition.

Conclusion

The Lean and Mathlib communities’ endorsement is strong evidence that the formal statement and proof passed their checks. The next stage is mathematical understanding, publication and broader acceptance. The Riemann hypothesis AI proof marks an important moment for AI-assisted research, but readers should not confuse formal verification with a prize award or a completed human review process.

Frequently Asked Questions

Has the Riemann hypothesis been proved?

The Lean and Mathlib communities say Alpöge’s formal proof and statement are correct. Broader mathematical review and Clay prize recognition remain separate.

Did Claude prove it alone?

No. Levent Alpöge says he worked with Claude. The research claim is attributed to the mathematician and his AI-assisted process.

What is Lean?

Lean is a proof assistant that lets mathematicians encode formal statements and proofs for a small kernel to check.

Why does the Clay Institute still list the problem as unsolved?

The Institute’s prize process requires qualifying publication, two years and general acceptance. The verification statement does not itself award the prize.

What is the difference between formal verification and peer review?

Formal verification checks a proof against encoded definitions and axioms. Peer review and mathematical exposition assess the argument’s significance, clarity and place in the literature.
