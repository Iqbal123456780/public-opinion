US AI Chip Export Approvals to China Face New Congressional Scrutiny

Congressional records show nearly 880,000 advanced AI chips approved for China, raising questions about licensing, safeguards and national security.

Published November 20, 2026, 8:15 AM PKT · Public Opinion News Editorial Team

Newly released US licensing records have intensified debate over the country's AI chip export policy. The House Foreign Affairs Committee said the Commerce Department approved 879,868 advanced chips for Chinese companies across applications covering December 2025 through May 2026, while requests exceeded 3.4 million. The disclosure does not establish that every approved chip was delivered. The scrutiny of AI chip exports to China focuses on national security, customer screening and safeguards for advanced computing hardware. The records cover Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X products, and lawmakers are asking whether reviews adequately assessed recipients and possible diversion risks.

1. What the congressional disclosure says

The House Foreign Affairs Committee released license information on November 19. Chairman Brian Mast said the records showed requests for more than 3.4 million advanced AI chips and approvals for 879,868. The disclosure was made under the Export Control Reform Act, according to the committee's statement.

The numbers refer to license approvals, not a verified tally of chips delivered to China. The committee's records list applications and decisions, and some entries were marked approved but not shipped as of the date in the documents. The AI chip exports to China issue therefore needs careful distinction between authorisation, shipment and final use.

2. Which chips were involved

The disclosure covered Nvidia H200 accelerators and AMD's MI325X, both designed for high-performance computing and AI workloads. Such hardware can support large model training, inference, scientific computing and data-centre operations. Their performance and memory capacity make them strategically important in the technology competition.

The committee's summary said the applications covered several Chinese companies and included approvals for Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu and BYD. The figures are based on released records and do not prove that every recipient obtained the chips. Nor do they establish the exact end use of each authorised shipment.

3. Requests were larger than approvals

Companies had applied to ship more than 3.4 million covered chips, while the committee reported 879,868 approvals. The gap cannot be described as a list of denied shipments without additional information. Applications may be pending, withdrawn, revised or subject to other decisions.

The distinction matters for export-control analysis. Approval counts show what regulators authorised within the period, but they do not reveal the full number of chips that crossed borders. The AI chip exports to China debate will require shipment records, compliance audits and information about whether license conditions were met.

4. Why customer screening matters

The committee highlighted approvals involving companies it said were designated by the Department of War as Chinese Military Companies. Such designations can trigger scrutiny, but a designation does not automatically establish that a particular chip shipment was used for military purposes or that a license violated the law.

Export rules are applied to specific products, recipients, end users and transactions. Analysts must review the applicable regulations and license conditions before drawing conclusions. The disclosure raises questions about the review process, but it is not a judicial finding that approved exports were unlawful.

5. How US policy changed

The US revised its license-review policy for certain advanced computing commodities in January 2026, moving specified products from a presumption of denial to case-by-case consideration under conditions. The Federal Register rule includes supply assurances, security procedures and independent testing requirements.

The policy change helps explain why some H200-equivalent applications could be considered. It does not mean all sales are automatically allowed. The AI chip exports to China debate is now about whether the conditions were sufficient, whether the agencies applied them consistently and whether national-security risks were properly assessed.

6. What Nvidia and AMD say

Reuters reported that Nvidia said approvals were subject to strict conditions and limited volumes, and that it had stopped H200 shipments to China. AMD said it complies with export-control laws. These company statements are relevant, but they do not independently verify the delivery status of every licensed chip.

The companies have commercial incentives to protect access to markets while complying with regulations. Their statements should be read alongside government records and the licensing rules. A license is not a guarantee of shipment, and a shipment does not by itself prove that all conditions were satisfied.

7. The diversion and smuggling concern

The committee also raised questions about possible intermediaries and diversion risks. Advanced chips can be attractive to buyers seeking access to computing power without directly purchasing from a controlled supplier. The risk is why licensing, customer verification and end-use monitoring matter.

The release contains allegations and oversight questions, not proof that all approved transactions involved diversion. Regulators must distinguish legitimate commercial data-centre use from prohibited transfers. The AI chip exports to China controversy will likely focus on whether screening identified risky buyers and whether post-export monitoring was adequate.

8. Why the issue matters for the AI race

Advanced accelerators are a key input for training and running large AI models. Restrictions can shape the cost, scale and speed of computing available to Chinese firms, while US suppliers face pressure to balance national-security rules with commercial demand.

China is also investing in domestic chips and software. Export controls may limit access to some advanced hardware, but they can encourage substitution, efficiency improvements and local production. The long-term outcome will depend on technological progress, supply chains and policy choices rather than a single licensing disclosure.

9. Congressional oversight and next steps

The House committee's disclosure is likely to lead to further questions for the Commerce Department. Lawmakers may seek explanations of license review standards, customer checks, shipment records and the conditions attached to approvals. The committee's public statement does not itself change the export rules.

The AI chip exports to China issue could become a test of how Congress balances executive licensing authority with oversight. Future hearings, agency responses and additional documents may clarify the record. Any new restriction would require a separate official action, not simply a committee criticism.

10. What the records do and do not prove

The disclosed records show that nearly 880,000 advanced chips were approved across the specified applications, and that requests were much larger. They also identify products, recipients and licensing decisions. They do not establish how many chips were delivered, whether all approvals were used or whether a violation occurred.

A sound assessment will compare the documents with the applicable rules, company statements and any subsequent enforcement findings. The AI chip exports to China debate is significant because it involves powerful hardware and sensitive end users, but the facts should remain separate from political allegations until further evidence is released.

Bottom line: The records create a new oversight question for Washington, but approvals should not be confused with deliveries or proof of unlawful transfers.
