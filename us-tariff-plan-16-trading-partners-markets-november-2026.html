US Tariff Plan Could Hit 16 Major Trading Partners

Washington is preparing new duties after a trade probe, while markets weigh tariff risks, AI stock losses and key U.S. inflation data.

Published November 25, 2026 · By Public Opinion News Editorial Team

The White House is preparing a new round of tariffs that could affect imports from 16 major U.S. trading partners, according to people cited by Reuters. The announcement was expected imminently on Wednesday, but final rates and product lists had not been published at the time of this report. The proposed duties follow a U.S. investigation into excess industrial capacity and arrive as markets are unsettled by a technology-stock selloff. The US tariff plan could affect manufacturers, importers and shoppers, but the size of any price impact will depend on the final rules, exemptions and how companies respond.

What the administration is preparing

Reuters reported that the administration intends to address what it calls excess industrial capacity among 16 major trading partners. Three people familiar with the matter said an announcement was expected soon. The report did not identify a final schedule of rates or a complete list of products that would be covered. That distinction matters. A proposal or expected announcement is not the same as a tariff that has taken legal effect. Businesses still need the official notice, effective dates and product codes before they can calculate their exposure.

Which trading partners are under review

The economies named in the Reuters report were China, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Switzerland, Norway, Singapore, Bangladesh, Cambodia and the European Union. The list covers a wide range of manufacturing and supply-chain hubs. It includes large producers of electronics, vehicles, machinery, metals and consumer goods. The U.S. Trade Representative's March investigation also identified those 16 economies. The US tariff plan does not mean every product from every partner will receive the same duty.

Why the legal route matters

The investigation was launched under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. That law allows the U.S. Trade Representative to investigate foreign acts, policies or practices that may burden U.S. commerce. The administration argues that persistent excess capacity can displace U.S. production and discourage investment. The USTR's original notice listed manufacturing sectors such as semiconductors, steel, autos, batteries and machinery. The US tariff plan is therefore being framed as a response to industrial policy, not simply as a broad import tax.

Rates may differ by country and trade deal

Reuters reported that the duties were expected to be tailored by country and that existing trade-deal caps would be honored. The people cited said ceilings included 15% for goods from the European Union, Japan and South Korea, and 19% for Malaysia. They also said the China rate was expected to stay within the current truce range if that arrangement is extended. These are reported expectations, not final tariff schedules. The White House and USTR had not responded to Reuters' questions about the pending announcement.

Why markets reacted before the announcement

Investors were already reducing exposure to technology shares when tariff news added another source of uncertainty. Reuters reported that, at 6:01 a.m. Eastern time, Nasdaq 100 futures were down 2.38%, S&P 500 futures were down 1.44% and Dow futures were down 0.76%. Nvidia, Micron, AMD and Marvell were among the chipmakers under pressure. Those figures are a premarket snapshot, not the final performance of the indexes at the close. The US tariff plan was one factor in a wider market pullback.

How tariffs can reach U.S. shoppers

Import duties are paid by U.S. importers when covered goods enter the country. Companies can respond in several ways: absorb some of the cost, negotiate lower supplier prices, shift production or raise prices for customers. The final result depends on competition, inventory, exchange rates and how easily a product can be sourced elsewhere. A tariff on a component can also affect a finished product assembled in the United States. That is why businesses examine the full supply chain rather than only the country printed on a retail label.

The China trade truce adds another layer

The Reuters report said the existing U.S.-China trade truce runs until January 10 and that the administration was expected to honor the agreed ceiling while the arrangement remains in place. It also reported that the two governments were discussing lower tariffs on $30 billion of non-strategic imports from each side. Beijing rejected the U.S. argument about excess capacity and said the issue should not be used for political manipulation. A new duty could therefore affect trade talks as well as individual companies.

What manufacturers and importers should watch

Companies need to know which tariff classification applies to each product, where key components originate and whether an exemption is available. A manufacturer may source raw materials in one country, assemble in another and sell in the United States. The legal origin of an imported item can depend on customs rules, not only the last shipping location. The US tariff plan may prompt firms to review contracts, inventory and delivery schedules. Businesses should avoid expensive changes until official rates and effective dates are clear.

Inflation and Federal Reserve data remain important

Investors were also waiting for the Bureau of Economic Analysis to release October personal income and outlays and the second estimate of third-quarter GDP at 8:30 a.m. Eastern time. The latest available PCE report, for September, showed the price index rising 3.6% from a year earlier and core PCE rising 3.1%. Those figures were the previous release, not the October data. The Federal Reserve watches PCE inflation closely, so the new report could influence expectations for interest rates.

What happens next: official rates, dates and FAQs

The next step is the administration's formal announcement and any Federal Register notice. Readers should look for the tariff authority, covered products, country-specific rates, exclusions and start dates. Until those details are published, companies and consumers should treat the US tariff plan as pending rather than an implemented charge. Have the new tariffs taken effect?

Not at the time of this report. Reuters said an announcement was expected imminently, but final rates and dates had not been published.

Which countries are named?

The Reuters report listed 16 economies, including China, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam and the European Union.

Will every product face the same rate?

No. Sources cited by Reuters said rates would be tailored by country and existing trade-deal caps respected.

Who pays a tariff at the border?

The U.S. importer of record generally pays the duty. The cost may then be shared across suppliers, companies and customers.

What should consumers expect?

Price effects are uncertain until the final product list, rates, exemptions and effective dates are known.

Bottom line: The announcement could reshape supply-chain decisions, but the actual economic effect will depend on the rules Washington publishes. Investors should separate market reaction from confirmed policy.
